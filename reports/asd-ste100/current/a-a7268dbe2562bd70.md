## Executive summary

- The authors derive maximum likelihood alignment of two DNA sequences from a statistical model with explicit transition probabilities. (PDF p. 1).
- Their parameter-estimation method sums likelihoods over possible alignments. They compare it with methods that use a single alignment. (PDF p. 2).

## Objectives

- The authors aim to adjust alignment weights to evolutionary rates and divergence times instead of choosing fixed or subjective weights. (PDF p. 2).
- They examine bias in evolutionary parameter estimates obtained from one selected alignment. (PDF p. 2).

## Arguments

- The authors argue that evolutionary event weights should depend on event probabilities. These probabilities depend on the sequence pair. (PDF p. 2).
- They attribute single-alignment bias to repeated selection of one event explanation when substitutions and indels are both plausible. (PDF p. 8).

## Analysis

- Reversibility lets the pair likelihood be calculated by treating one observed sequence as ancestral to the other. This removes an explicit sum over ancestral sequences. (PDF p. 2).
- The direct alignment approach requires computation proportional to I times the product of sequence lengths, where I is the number of alignment iterations. (PDF p. 8).
- The likelihood surface for a selected best alignment is not differentiable everywhere. The authors describe the summed likelihood surface as differentiable and easier to optimize. (PDF p. 8).

## Methods

- The model separates substitution from insertion and deletion. Its reversible substitution process draws replacement bases from equilibrium nucleotide probabilities. (PDF p. 3).
- Indels follow an independent link birth-death process. A sequence has one normal link per base and one immortal link. Birth adds one base; normal-link death removes one base. (PDF p. 3).
- The sum approach estimates parameters from the total likelihood across alignments. The indirect approach alternates parameter optimization for a fixed alignment with selection of a new best alignment. (PDF p. 8).

## Applications

- The authors apply the methods to mitochondrial aspartic-acid tRNA genes from mouse and ox. The sum and direct approaches produce the same reported alignment. (PDF p. 10).

## Optimization

- The indirect approach separates fixed-alignment parameter optimization from alignment search. Fixed-alignment likelihood costs O(max(sA,sB)), while alignment search costs O(sA sB). (PDF p. 8).
- The indirect approach stops when the new alignment equals the previous alignment. The authors report fewer alignment iterations than for the direct approach. (PDF p. 8).

## Evaluation and testing

- The authors compare sample standard errors from simulated replicate sequence pairs with asymptotic estimates obtained from the inverse information matrix. (PDF p. 8).
- A sequence-length experiment uses ancestral lengths of 250, 500, and 1,000 bases. It estimates standard errors from 20 sequence pairs at μt = 0.1 and st = 0.5. (PDF p. 10).
- A simulated example with ancestral length 500 compares the parameter estimates and resulting alignments from the sum and direct approaches. (PDF p. 10).

## Results

- All three approaches perform similarly for closely related sequences. The accuracy advantage of the sum approach increases with evolutionary distance, while single-alignment estimates show increasing bias. (PDF p. 8).
- The authors report that the indirect approach is substantially faster than the other two approaches. They give no timing value in this excerpt. (PDF p. 8).
- For one example with true μt = 0.1 and st = 0.5, the sum approach estimates 0.115 and 0.465. The direct approach estimates 0.031 and 0.573. (PDF p. 10).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- The model permits only substitutions and single-base insertions and deletions. It excludes inversions and large indel events. (PDF p. 2).
- The authors have no theoretical convergence guarantee for the indirect approach, although they report no observed convergence failure. (PDF p. 8).
- The algorithms treat terminal gaps as evolutionary events. The authors identify missing sequence data, rate variation, and sequence-context effects as issues for future extensions. (PDF p. 11).
