## Executive summary

- The authors organize pairwise and multiple sequence aligners by common algorithmic stages. Their literature collection contains 158 documents. (PDF p. 3).
- The authors find that varied application requirements and benchmark datasets make a standard evaluation protocol difficult to establish. (PDF p. 31).

## Objectives

- The authors aim to connect aligners with their complete algorithmic protocols and basic characteristics. (PDF p. 3).
- The review examines alignment quality, performance, replicability, reliability, and validity across methods from three decades. (PDF p. 3).

## Arguments

- The authors argue that common algorithmic stages help researchers compare aligners and locate specific computational features. (PDF p. 3).
- The authors argue that speed and completeness can impose conflicting requirements. They state that each aligner addresses particular needs. (PDF p. 31).

## Analysis

- The review describes Needleman–Wunsch as a global alignment method and Smith–Waterman as a local alignment method. It reports time and memory proportional to the product of sequence lengths. (PDF p. 9).
- The review contrasts optimal dynamic programming with faster word-based heuristics. Seed-and-extend methods restrict the regions that require expensive alignment computation. (PDF p. 9).
- The reviewed genetic methods can require substantial computation because of repeated iterations. Their outcomes depend on sequence length, sequence count, encoding, and the fitness function. (PDF p. 29).

## Methods

- The authors use a systematic literature review of Scopus, PubMed, and Web of Science. They also analyze publication metadata for citation, location, institution, and temporal trends. (PDF p. 3).
- The Clustal W protocol comprises pairwise alignment, a percent-identity distance matrix, a neighbour-joining guide tree, and progressive alignment of larger sequence groups. (PDF p. 16).
- The review describes iterative refinement as splitting an alignment into two groups and realigning them until convergence. MUSCLE uses profile-based group alignment. (PDF p. 18).
- The genetic algorithm framework creates an alignment population, evaluates an objective function, selects fitter alignments, and applies crossover and mutation. (PDF p. 18).

## Applications

- Sequence alignment supports evolutionary analysis, functional-region identification, protein structure and function prediction, and drug or diagnostic target identification. (PDF p. 31).

## Optimization

- The review describes MAFFT's use of Fourier transforms to accelerate alignment and PASTA's parallel strategy for large alignments. (PDF p. 18).
- PRRP uses hill climbing with weighted sum-of-pairs scoring. It iteratively adjusts alignment weights and locally divergent or gap-rich regions. (PDF p. 18).
- The review describes diversity control in quantum-behaved particle swarm optimization as a way to improve global search during profile HMM training. (PDF p. 30).

## Evaluation and testing

- The review identifies BAliBASE, Pfam, and HOMSTRAD as structural benchmarks. It identifies ROSE, INDELible, and Dawg as sources of simulated sequence datasets. (PDF p. 29).
- Sum-of-pairs and true-column scores compare aligned residue pairs and columns. Friedman and Wilcoxon tests are described as statistical ranking tools. (PDF p. 29).
- The review describes MUMSA as an evaluation approach based on agreement among alignments. It discusses multiple-overlap and head-or-tail scores. (PDF p. 29).

## Results

- The authors report that common stages expose shared algorithmic features and support comparison across a broad collection of aligners. (PDF p. 31).
- The review reports that QPSO and its diversity-controlled variant improved benchmark performance for profile HMM training compared with Baum–Welch and classical PSO. The excerpt gives no numerical effect sizes. (PDF p. 30).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- Structural benchmarks use fixed protein-structure test sets. Simulated datasets depend on probabilistic models that can have deficiencies. (PDF p. 29).
- The authors warn that agreement among several alignment methods can still produce incorrect conclusions. (PDF p. 29).
- Different benchmark datasets reduce confidence in comparisons between reported aligner performance results. (PDF p. 31).
- Optical methods require complex hardware. The review also notes that basic moiré methods compare only two sequences at a time. (PDF p. 31).
