## Executive summary

- ProTrek learns a shared representation of protein sequence, structure, and function text through bidirectional contrastive learning. (PDF p. 1).
- The model supports nine retrieval directions within and between its three modalities. Inference uses maximum inner product search. (PDF p. 3).
- The authors report improved protein–text retrieval against ProteinDT and ProtST on a Swiss-Prot benchmark. (PDF p. 3).

## Objectives

- The authors aim to connect protein sequence, structure, and function for searches that can use any of these modalities. (PDF p. 1).
- The authors aim to transfer structural and functional information into sequence representations for downstream prediction tasks. (PDF p. 3).

## Arguments

- The authors argue that single-modality alignment tools restrict discovery of relationships between sequence, structure, and function. (PDF p. 1).
- The authors argue that global representations can identify functional similarity despite divergent sequences and structures. (PDF p. 3).

## Analysis

- Interpretation: ProTrek's representation alignment brings related embeddings together. The described retrieval procedure does not construct a residue-level sequence alignment. (PDF p. 1).
- The authors attribute much of the retrieval improvement to a training dataset about two orders of magnitude larger than those of ProteinDT and ProtST. (PDF p. 3).
- A comparison of 4,000 sampled protein pairs found a correlation between structure-to-sequence ProTrek scores and TM-scores. (PDF p. 13).

## Methods

- The model uses an ESM sequence encoder and a BERT text encoder. Genuine modality pairs are brought closer in embedding space, while negative pairs are separated. (PDF p. 1).
- Foldseek converts structures into discrete tokens for a BERT-style structure encoder. Training combines six directional contrastive losses with two masked language modeling losses. (PDF p. 2).
- InfoNCE uses cosine similarity and a learned temperature initialized to 0.07. Other pairs in each batch serve as negative examples. (PDF p. 11).
- Training combines 14 million Swiss-Prot protein–text pairs with 25 million filtered TrEMBL50 pairs. An initial model filters a pool of 300 million noisy pairs. (PDF p. 11).

## Applications

- ProTrek supports protein retrieval from natural-language descriptions and function-description retrieval from proteins. (PDF p. 3).
- The authors propose structure-to-sequence scores for screening inverse-folding candidates before further structure prediction. (PDF p. 13).
- The authors retrieve enzyme descriptions from a curated EC text database and apply a maximum-separation rule for annotation. (PDF p. 15).
- The study uses sequence and text queries to retrieve uracil-DNA glycosylase candidates from OMG prot50 for experimental analysis. (PDF p. 16).

## Optimization

- Maximum inner product search enables retrieval from precomputed embeddings. Large and compact encoder variants support different resource requirements. (PDF p. 3).
- The initial 35M model scores noisy training pairs. Only pairs above the mean Swiss-Prot score enter the expanded training set. (PDF p. 11).
- The authors split long function descriptions into sentences before GPT-4 paraphrasing. This reduces omissions observed with longer inputs. (PDF p. 11).

## Evaluation and testing

- The retrieval benchmark uses 4,000 Swiss-Prot proteins with less than 50% sequence identity to training proteins. It adds 100,000 UniProt proteins as unknown negatives and compares ProteinDT and ProtST. (PDF p. 3).
- An all-versus-all test counts a retrieved protein as correct when it shares at least one GO annotation with the query. MAP measures the ranks of relevant retrieval results. (PDF p. 13).
- For 11 downstream tasks, structural datasets use a 70% LDDT similarity threshold for partitioning. Mutation datasets retain their published splits and experimental settings. (PDF p. 16).
- The experimental workflow tests retrieved candidates in HeLa cells and sequences target amplicons. CRISPResso2 aligns reads and quantifies substitutions and indels. (PDF p. 16).

## Results

- Sequence-to-text MAP is 0.233 for ProTrek, 0.007 for ProtST, and 0.004 for ProteinDT. Text-to-sequence MAP is 0.190, 0.001, and 0.003, respectively. (PDF p. 3).
- The reported hit rate is 95.33% for single-category text queries and 65.83% for queries with two functions or properties. (PDF p. 3).
- The authors report enzyme annotation performance comparable to CLEAN on datasets released after ProTrek's training-data collection. (PDF p. 3).
- The authors report broadly comparable downstream performance for ProTrek and ESM-3. They state that ESM-3 has twice as many parameters. (PDF p. 13).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- Training excludes proteins without predicted structures. (PDF p. 11).
- The authors state that agreement with UniProt annotations alone does not establish accurate prediction of actual protein properties. (PDF p. 15).
- The convergent-function retrieval evaluation uses annotation keywords and EC hierarchy matches. Many entries are unreviewed, so the authors treat the results as preliminary. (PDF p. 15).
- The authors suggest that encoder size and pretraining differences may explain weaker structure-encoder performance. They do not establish this explanation. (PDF p. 3).

It is involved in several metabolic pathways, including the pentose phosphate pathway, starch and sucrose metabolism, and amino sugar metabolism. Glucose-6-phosphate isomerase is found in a wide range of organisms, including archaea, bacteria, and eukaryotes, and it contributes to the efficient use of carbohydrates in cellular metabolism. EC 6.1.1.1 The enzyme, Tyrosine-tRNA ligase (EC 6.1.1.1), is a ligase that catalyzes the reaction between ATP, L-tyrosine, and tRNA(Tyr) to form L-tyrosyl-tRNA(Tyr), AMP, and diphosphate. This enzyme plays a critical role in protein synthesis by linking tyrosine to its corresponding tRNA, enabling its incorporation into growing polypeptide chains. It is classified under ligases that form carbon-oxygen bonds, specifically aminoacyl-tRNA ligases. Tyrosine-tRNA ligase is involved in the aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis pathway, essential for accurate translation of the genetic code. It is integral to the immune response against invading genetic material but is less commonly applied in gene-editing applications due to its complex mechanism. Its dual function enables it to unwind and cleave DNA, contributing to the degradation pathway essential for microbial defense. CRISPR-Cas9 Cas9 is part of the Type II CRISPR-Cas systems, functioning as a single-protein system that specifically targets DNA. It was initially discovered in Streptococcus pyogenes and is renowned for its ability to make precise cuts in DNA at target sites defined by guide RNA. Cas9 generates blunt ends when it cuts DNA, making it highly suitable for creating gene knockouts or insertions at specific locations. The ease of designing guide RNAs for Cas9 allows for targeted genome editing with high specificity, enabling applications across a wide range of organisms. Its reliability and precision have made Cas9 one of the most widely used tools in genetic engineering, biotechnology, and therapeutic research. CRISPR-Cas10 Cas10 is part of the Type III CRISPR-Cas systems, serving as the signature protein that facilitates complex antiviral defense mechanisms. It is a multi-domain protein that plays a crucial role in targeting both RNA and, indirectly, DNA. Cas10 is unique in its dual activity, where it binds CRISPR RNA (crRNA) to guide the recognition of target RNA and induces transcription-coupled DNA cleavage through an auxiliary complex. This capability allows Cas10 to participate in more intricate gene regulation processes, including immune response signaling and the modulation of foreign genetic material. The multifunctionality of Cas10 has made it an intriguing subject of study, especially for applications that require a combined approach to RNA and DNA interference. Its catalytic versatility extends the potential use of Type III CRISPR systems in advanced genetic engineering and synthetic biology research. CRISPR-Cas12 Cas12 is part of the Type V CRISPR-Cas systems, functioning as a single-protein system that specifically targets DNA. It was initially discovered in organisms such as Acidaminococcus and Lachnospiraceae. Cas12 generates staggered (sticky) ends when it cuts DNA, which facilitates the insertion of specific sequences and can be beneficial for DNA repair. An additional unique feature of Cas12 is its ability to independently process CRISPR RNA (crRNA) from a precursor transcript. This capability enables Cas12 to perform multiplex gene editing efficiently, making it especially useful in complex genetic engineering applications that require editing multiple genes at once. Its distinctive cutting mechanism and versatility in DNA editing have made Cas12 a powerful tool in biotechnological and therapeutic fields. CRISPR-Cas13 Cas13 is a distinctive member of the Type VI CRISPR-Cas systems, notable for its ability to exclusively target and cleave RNA rather than DNA. This protein operates by recognizing specific RNA sequences using guide RNA (crRNA) and initiating precise RNA cleavage, accompanied by collateral RNA degradation. Cas13’s unique RNA-targeting mechanism makes it a powerful tool for RNA editing, gene expression regulation, and antiviral applications. CRISPR Csm/Cmr Complex The Csm/Cmr complex is part of the Type III CRISPR-Cas systems, functioning as a multi- protein system that can target both DNA and RNA. It is commonly found in various bacteria and archaea, including thermophiles. The Csm/Cmr complex operates by recognizing and degrad- ing foreign genetic material from viruses or plasmids, contributing to cellular immune defense. Its unique ability to target both types of nucleic acids allows it to defend against a wider range of threats. This dual functionality makes the Csm/Cmr complex an important model for understand- ing immune defense mechanisms and offers potential applications in biotechnological fields that require simultaneous RNA and DNA targeting. The “Protein existence = 1” condition exclusively includes testing set proteins classified as “Experimental evidence at protein level” (UniProt PE1) and excludes annotations containing the phrase “By similarity.” This ensures that all ground-truth annotations used for evaluation are derived solely from clear experimental evidence, rather than homology-based inferences. The “All” condition uses the original testing set for a comparison purpose. For the nearest neighbor paradigm, the function predicted for a test protein is assigned using the description derived from its nearest neighbor which is calculated based on the cosine similarity in the ESM-2 embedding space. For ProTrek protein-to-text search, we only consider the top-1 searched results for a fair comparison. The use of a test set restricted to proteins with ‘Protein Existence = 1’ (PE1) as a more rigorous gold standard is primarily to mitigate a potential evaluation bias that arises when directly comparing ProTrek’s protein-to-text search with a nearest-neighbor approach. The fundamental issue stems from the fact that approximately 80% of protein annotations in Swiss-Prot are not derived from clear experimental evidence but are instead inferred using methods such as ISS (inferred from sequence or structural similarity) with human review. This annotation methodology is conceptually aligned with the nearest neighbor approach. Given that the annotations for non-PE1 proteins in Swiss-Prot are not 100% accurate (see PMCID: PMC1892569), using a test set that includes these proteins creates an unfair setting for this evaluation scenario. As illustrated in this figure, ProTrek’s protein-to-text search demonstrates a clear advantage on the PE1 test set. In contrast, on the “All” set (which includes a large volume of non-PE1 proteins), the nearest neighbor methods may appear better results (e.g. on the “Pathway” and “Function” prediction task). However, this better performance does not necessarily indicate the superiority of the NN method.
