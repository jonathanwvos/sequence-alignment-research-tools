## Executive summary

- The authors study LCSk, which maximizes the number of ordered matching length-k substrings that do not overlap in either input. (PDF p. 1).
- They give dynamic programming, sparse, dense, and tabulation algorithms. All use linear-time preprocessing to find matching length-k substrings. (PDF p. 1).

## Objectives

- The authors aim to obtain O(n²) time without a constant-k restriction and to improve this bound with techniques adapted from LCS algorithms. (PDF p. 1).

## Arguments

- LCSk needs access to threshold states from k rows earlier. Naive copies add an O(nℓ) time term, so the sparse algorithm uses persistence. (PDF p. 3).
- The dense algorithm skips matches that cannot change the result. It uses the separation of at least k cells between relevant increments. (PDF p. 3).

## Analysis

- Lemma 1 states that each DP cell gives the LCSk value for its two input prefixes. The final cell therefore gives the required length. (PDF p. 2).
- The sparse algorithm processes r matches with O(log ℓ) threshold operations per match. Here, ℓ ≤ n/k is the solution length. (PDF p. 3).
- The dense analysis separates rows by match density. It balances preprocessing costs with block size b = √t₂ and threshold t₂ = (k log n)^(2/3). (PDF p. 4).

## Methods

- Preprocessing builds a suffix array and LCP table for B#A. It groups suffixes with a shared length-k prefix and radix-sorts positions to provide match lists. (PDF p. 2).
- The basic DP uses neighboring prefix values and a jump of k positions in both inputs for a matching length-k substring. (PDF p. 2).
- LCSk-Sparse stores threshold versions in a persistent red-black tree. LCSk-Dense uses threshold arrays and successor queries on match lists. (PDF p. 3).
- DP-4R encodes row increments and matches as bit vectors. A lookup table computes snippets of Θ(log n) bits from earlier rows and two boundary differences. (PDF p. 4).

## Applications

- The authors identify computational biology as the motivation for matching non-overlapping substrings instead of individual symbols. (PDF p. 1).

## Optimization

- Linear preprocessing permits constant time per reported match. Scanning these lists removes the factor k from the basic quadratic DP time. (PDF p. 2).
- The dense algorithm reuses successor answers for duplicate rows. It stores either one answer or a full answer array for each block. (PDF p. 4).
- A variant uses van Emde Boas trees with lazy initialization. It supports successor queries in O(log log n) time. (PDF p. 4).
- DP-4R can build match bit vectors one row at a time to avoid O(n²) bits of match storage. (PDF p. 4).

## Results

- The basic DP takes O(n²) time and O(nk) space. LCSk-Sparse takes O(n + r log ℓ) time and O(n + min(r, nℓ)) space. (PDF p. 2).
- LCSk-Dense takes O(n²/k + n(k log n)^(2/3)) time and O(n) space. (PDF p. 2).
- The van Emde Boas variant takes O(n² log log n/k) time and O(n log log n) space. (PDF p. 2).
- DP-4R takes O(n²/log n) time and O(n + nk/log n) words of space. Sequence extraction needs O(n²/log n) extra space. (PDF p. 2).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- The sparse method can have a superquadratic bound when the match count is Θ(n²). The dense method addresses this regime. (PDF p. 3).
- The authors could not obtain an attractive adaptation of dominant-match LCS methods. Temporary removal of matches across k rows and columns complicates these methods. (PDF p. 4).
- The authors conjecture that an O(n²/w) bit-logic algorithm may be difficult or impossible. They do not establish an impossibility result. (PDF p. 5).
