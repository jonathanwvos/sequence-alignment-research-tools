## Executive summary

- The authors generate molecular surfaces with a Clifford–Fourier transform (CFT) and a PDE transform. They test piecewise and Gaussian initial data. (PDF p. 1).
- Validation includes surface comparisons, electrostatic potential maps, and solvation free energies for 21 proteins. (PDF p. 10).

## Objectives

- Develop a geometric algebra algorithm that defines biomolecular surfaces from atomic coordinates and radii. (PDF p. 2).

## Arguments

- The authors identify geometric singularities in common molecular surfaces as a source of computational difficulty. (PDF p. 2).
- The authors choose the 3D CFT because its derivative property supports PDE solution. They explain that pseudoscalar noncommutativity prevents an equivalent general derivative rule in 2D. (PDF p. 13).

## Analysis

- Longer propagation times smooth the three-atom test surfaces and remove singularities. Excessive propagation also removes useful shape detail. (PDF p. 6).
- For protein 1ajj, larger isovalues expand piecewise surfaces and contract Gaussian surfaces. Changing the piecewise isovalue does not remove singularities. (PDF p. 9).

## Methods

- The algorithm constructs initial data from atomic coordinates and van der Waals radii. It solves a linearized high-order PDE through a CFT frequency response and extracts an isosurface. (PDF p. 6).
- Piecewise initial data assign zero inside atomic spheres and one outside. Gaussian initial data use the maximum of atom-centered Gaussian functions, with a scale parameter of 3 Å. (PDF p. 6).
- The authors obtain protein structures from the PDB. PDB2PQR adds missing hydrogen atoms and assigns atomic charges with the CHARMM force field. (PDF p. 9).

## Applications

- APBS in VMD computes Poisson–Boltzmann electrostatic potentials for mapping onto CFT and MSMS surfaces. (PDF p. 10).
- MIBPB computes electrostatic solvation free energies for comparison with MSMS and two FRI surface methods. (PDF p. 10).

## Optimization

- The fast CFT reduces the computational cost of the high-order PDE transform. (PDF p. 6).
- The authors attribute time efficiency to obtaining the terminal state in one step. (PDF p. 13).

## Evaluation and testing

- A synthetic molecule has three atoms of radius 1.8 Å. Tests vary propagation time from 10 to 100,000 and vary isovalues for both initial data types. (PDF p. 6).
- Surface comparisons use proteins 1ajj, 1bor, 1mbg, and 1sh1. CFT settings use PDE order 12 and time 100, with isovalues 0.9 for piecewise data and 0.8 for Gaussian data. (PDF p. 10).
- The MSMS comparison uses probe radius 1.5 and density 9.5. Potential maps are compared for 1ajj and 1bor. (PDF p. 10).
- The 21-protein energy comparison uses CFT grid size 0.25, isovalue 0.9, and propagation time 100. (PDF p. 12).

## Results

- The authors report close visual agreement between CFT and MSMS potential maps for 1ajj and 1bor. They report smoother CFT surfaces without geometric singularities in these examples. (PDF p. 10).
- For 1ajj, CFT and MSMS solvation energies are −1154.039 and −1100.754 kcal/mol. For 1bor, they are −833.647 and −927.310 kcal/mol. (PDF p. 12).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- High propagation times can produce surfaces that are too smooth to be useful. (PDF p. 6).
- Isovalue selection must balance surface volume against geometric singularities. (PDF p. 10).
