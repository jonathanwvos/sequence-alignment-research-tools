## Executive summary

- The authors encode binary data as DNA sequences with homopolymer runs of at most three bases. (PDF p. 1).
- The code rate is (2n−1)/n bits per nucleotide. At n = 10, the rate is 1.9 bits per nucleotide. (PDF p. 4).
- All sequences in the reported simulation had GC-content between 0.4 and 0.6. (PDF p. 4).

## Objectives

- The authors seek a high code rate with short homopolymer runs and GC-content near 0.5. (PDF p. 1).

## Arguments

- The authors identify homopolymer length and GC-content as factors that affect DNA synthesis and sequencing errors. (PDF p. 1).
- The authors argue that their block construction limits error propagation compared with iterative DNA fountain decoding. (PDF p. 1).

## Analysis

- The authors count legal sequences with powers of a 64-state adjacency matrix. States represent three-base sequences. (PDF p. 2).
- For 3 ≤ n ≤ 35, each three-base context permits at least 2^(2n−1) legal successor blocks. (PDF p. 2).
- The GC-content estimate treats block GC-content values as independent and identically distributed. Successor counts approximate block probabilities. (PDF p. 3).
- A multinomial distribution gives the estimated GC-content distribution of complete codewords. (PDF p. 4).

## Methods

- For each three-base context, the method orders legal successor blocks by distance from GC-content 0.5. Lexicographic order resolves ties. (PDF p. 3).
- Each context retains the first 2^(2n−1) blocks. A binary block of 2n−1 bits selects one entry. (PDF p. 3).
- The encoder concatenates k blocks. The last three bases of the previous block select the next mapping. (PDF p. 3).
- The decoder recovers each binary block from its position in the context-specific ordered set. Binary search takes O(n) time per block, as stated by the authors. (PDF p. 4).

## Applications

- The method maps digital data to synthetic DNA for storage and supports the inverse mapping after sequencing. (PDF p. 1).

## Optimization

- The block ordering gives priority to GC-content near 0.5 while retaining enough blocks for the required code rate. (PDF p. 3).
- Larger n increases the code rate but increases construction cost. The authors select n = 10 as a practical balance. (PDF p. 4).

## Evaluation and testing

- The simulation uses k = 20 and 100,000 random binary inputs for each n from 5 through 12. (PDF p. 4).
- Figure 2 compares estimated and empirical GC-content distributions for n = 8 and n = 10. (PDF p. 4).

## Results

- All tested codewords had GC-content between 0.4 and 0.6. (PDF p. 4).
- The authors report efficient set construction and encoding at n = 10, but give no measured runtime in this excerpt. (PDF p. 4).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- The GC-content model assumes independent blocks. The authors identify a Markov chain as a more accurate but more costly model. (PDF p. 3).
- Interpretation: the reported GC-content interval is supported by simulation, rather than a proof for every possible codeword. (PDF p. 4).
- Construction of the successor sets becomes time-consuming for large n, such as n ≥ 20. (PDF p. 4).
- The authors leave integration with Hamming distance and edit distance for future work. (PDF p. 4).
