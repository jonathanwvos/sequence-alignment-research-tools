## Executive summary

- FASTA joins compatible regions of sequence similarity to improve database search sensitivity. (PDF p. 1).
- RDF2 tests similarity scores with shuffled sequences. LFASTA reports multiple local alignments. (PDF p. 1).

## Objectives

- The authors seek a balance between sensitivity, selectivity, speed, and memory use. (PDF p. 1).
- The tools support database searches, significance tests, and detection of repeated sequence structures. (PDF p. 1).

## Arguments

- The authors argue that joining initial regions increases sensitivity with a small loss of selectivity and little loss of speed. (PDF p. 1).
- Global shuffling can remove local composition bias and make an observed similarity appear more significant. Local shuffling preserves this bias. (PDF p. 2).
- The authors prefer comparisons with the highest shuffled scores because FASTA shuffled scores can depart from a normal distribution. (PDF p. 5).

## Analysis

- FASTA can produce an initial score above its final optimized score. The initial calculation joins regions, but the final alignment uses a restricted band. (PDF p. 2).
- The authors attribute greater sensitivity in translated searches to amino acid scoring matrices that distinguish conservative from nonconservative substitutions. (PDF p. 3).

## Methods

- FASTA uses a lookup table to find runs of ktup identities. It selects ten diagonal regions, rescores them with a substitution matrix, and retains their highest scoring subregions. (PDF p. 1).
- LFASTA retains all diagonal regions above a threshold. For each region, it runs reverse and forward local alignment optimizations and reports an alignment or matrix plot. (PDF p. 2).
- RDF2 calculates single-region, joined-region, and optimized scores for shuffled sequences. Users can select global shuffling or shuffling within short blocks. (PDF p. 2).
- TFASTA translates database DNA in all six reading frames during the search. It includes introns and represents termination codons as unknown amino acids. (PDF p. 3).

## Applications

- The authors use LFASTA to compare chimpanzee globin mRNA with a rabbit globin gene and a nearby pseudogene. (PDF p. 3).
- A myosin heavy-chain self-comparison with PAM250 displays repeated protein structure. (PDF p. 5).

## Optimization

- FASTA selects a maximum-score combination of compatible initial regions with a joining penalty. A score threshold limits which regions can join. (PDF p. 1).
- Final alignment uses a modified Needleman–Wunsch and Smith–Waterman method within a band around the best initial region. (PDF p. 1).
- Users can change substitution scores and gap opening and extension values without changing the program. (PDF p. 3).

## Evaluation and testing

- The authors compare FASTA and FASTP with a T-cell receptor query against NBRF Release 12.0. They count known superfamily members among the top 200 hits. (PDF p. 3).
- A DNA search compares a 660-nucleotide rat growth-factor mRNA with mammalian sequences in GenBank Release 48, using ktup = 4. (PDF p. 3).
- RDF2 tests a growth-factor and ribosomal-RNA match with 50 shuffled comparisons and a local window of ten bases. (PDF p. 3).

## Results

- FASTA places 180 immunoglobulin superfamily members among the top 200 hits. FASTP places 160. (PDF p. 3).
- The rat growth-factor DNA search finishes in under 15 minutes on an IBM PC AT. (PDF p. 3).
- One shuffled initial score exceeds 140, and nine shuffled optimized scores exceed 107. The authors judge the growth-factor and ribosomal-RNA similarity unlikely to be significant. (PDF p. 3).
- The translated search ranks mouse epidermal growth factor above unrelated sequences. The DNA search does not place it among the highest scoring sequences. (PDF p. 3).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- The IBM PC versions limit query sequences to 2000 residues. (PDF p. 3).
- The translated search loses a human growth-factor fragment that contains only 13 translated codons and appears in the DNA search. (PDF p. 3).
- The supplied PDF page 4 contains an unrelated hemoglobin study. It cannot support notes on the sequence-comparison methods. (PDF p. 4).
