## Executive summary

- The authors relate codon usage to the Gibbs energy of codon–anticodon interactions. (PDF p. 1).
- They describe DNA thermodynamic properties per base pair as zero-order properties that depend on gross GC content. (PDF p. 3).
- They interpret codon bias as a strategy for efficiency and accuracy in gene expression. (PDF p. 1).

## Objectives

- The authors aim to assign a characteristic interaction Gibbs energy to each codon triplet and relate it to codon preference. (PDF p. 2).
- They aim to correlate physical and genetic maps of DNA. (PDF p. 9).
- Their final goal is to predict physical and associated genetic features from DNA sequence. (PDF p. 10).

## Arguments

- The authors argue that thermodynamic state functions permit energy comparisons without knowledge of the reaction mechanism. (PDF p. 1).
- They argue that the triplet is the relevant genetic unit in coding sequences and use it as the unit for thermodynamic analysis. (PDF p. 2).
- They interpret preferred bacterial codons of intermediate stability as a strategy that favors expression speed and efficiency. (PDF p. 7).

## Analysis

- At a fixed ionic strength, the authors report linear changes in melting temperature, enthalpy, entropy, and Gibbs energy with gross GC content. (PDF p. 3).
- The authors interpret these results as evidence that base-pair stability is independent of adjacent base pairs. (PDF p. 3).
- For eukaryotes, the reported intercodon CG frequency is 0.055, compared with an expected frequency of 0.11 from codon frequencies. (PDF p. 7).
- The authors argue that bacterial third-position codon choices offset GC variation in the first two positions and produce more uniform stability. (PDF p. 7).

## Methods

- The experimental approach follows DNA thermal denaturation through absorbance changes at 260 nm. Melting temperature marks the point where half the bases are unpaired. (PDF p. 2).
- The authors use Boltzmann’s formalism to estimate the entropy change from restricted backbone rotation. The estimate considers six bonds and three preferred conformations per bond. (PDF p. 2).
- Six synthetic DNA sequence types represent pure AT, pure GC, and mixed AT/GC environments to test neighbor effects on base-pair energetics. (PDF p. 3).
- The analysis compares GC content calculated from preferred codons with genomic GC content, including values derived from melting temperature. (PDF p. 7).

## Applications

- The authors propose that melting sub-transition patterns can identify related plasmids, inserted sequences, and effects on adjacent sequences. (PDF p. 9).
- They suggest that local thermal stability could help connect coding and signal sequences with physical DNA domains. They describe this evidence as preliminary. (PDF p. 9).

## Evaluation and testing

- The neighbor-effect test compares homopurine/homopyrimidine sequences with alternating purine/pyrimidine sequences and mixed AT/GC sequences. (PDF p. 3).
- Codon-preference analysis includes E. coli, B. subtilis, and T. ferrooxidans. It compares preferred-codon GC content with whole-genome GC content. (PDF p. 7).
- A cited survey of 165 E. coli genes reports a positive correlation between expression level and codon bias. (PDF p. 7).

## Results

- The authors report that a tenfold increase in cation concentration raises melting temperature by about 16 degrees. The relation is linear from millimolar concentrations to 0.3 molar. (PDF p. 3).
- Preferred-codon GC content is 48.2% for E. coli, 41.2% for B. subtilis, and 59% for T. ferrooxidans. The corresponding genomic values are about 50%, 42%, and 62%. (PDF p. 7).
- For human DNA, the authors report about 42% genomic GC content and 63% GC content calculated from preferred codons. (PDF p. 7).
- The authors describe plasmid melting curves as sums of sub-transitions from stability domains of roughly one hundred to several hundred base pairs. (PDF p. 9).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- The proposed relation between coding or signal sequences and local thermal stability rests on preliminary observations. (PDF p. 9).
- The authors state that further investigations are needed to strengthen the emerging evidence. (PDF p. 10).
- The authors leave the cause of CG avoidance in higher eukaryotes unresolved. They suggest a possible regulatory role for cytosine methylation. (PDF p. 7).
