## Executive summary

- PSIPRED predicts secondary structure with two neural networks. Its input is the position-specific scoring matrix generated by PSI-BLAST. (PDF p. 1).
- On 187 test chains, residue-weighted Q3 was 76.5% or 78.3%, depending on the mapping from DSSP states to three secondary structure states. (PDF p. 4).

## Objectives

- The author aims to make profile generation and secondary structure prediction easy to run on a workstation. (PDF p. 2).

## Arguments

- The author argues that direct use of PSI-BLAST profiles removes a costly separate multiple sequence alignment step. (PDF p. 2).
- The author argues that separating training and test proteins by fold reduces overlap that pairwise sequence screening can miss. (PDF p. 4).

## Analysis

- The author identifies reliable local alignments, iterative profile sensitivity and alignment accuracy as possible reasons for success. Their relative contributions were not established. (PDF p. 7).
- The simpler DSSP mapping assigns more residues to coil and gives a higher Q3 score. Thus, the reported accuracy depends on the reference state definition. (PDF p. 4).

## Methods

- PSI-BLAST runs for three iterations. Its final 20-by-sequence-length log-odds matrix is scaled to the range zero to one with a logistic function. (PDF p. 3).
- The first network uses a 15-residue window, 315 input units, 75 hidden units and three outputs for helix, strand and coil. Each position includes a chain terminus indicator. (PDF p. 4).
- A second network filters successive predictions. It uses a 15-position window with 60 input units and 60 hidden units. (PDF p. 4).
- Online back-propagation uses momentum 0.9 and learning rate 0.005. Training stops when performance declines on the 10% of training data reserved for monitoring. (PDF p. 4).

## Applications

- The author describes secondary structure predictions as constraints for tertiary structure prediction and as inputs to fold recognition. (PDF p. 1).

## Optimization

- The search database was filtered to remove low-information regions, transmembrane segments and likely coiled-coil regions. These steps aimed to reduce erroneous profile matches. (PDF p. 3).
- A limited architecture search used 16 CASP2 targets. A 15-residue window gave the best reported Q3 score of 80.1%. (PDF p. 4).
- Direct profile use reduced total prediction time. The author reports a two-minute prediction process on a Silicon Graphics Origin 200 server. (PDF p. 2).

## Evaluation and testing

- Three-way cross-validation used 187 protein chains with unique folds and structure resolution below 1.8 angstroms. The test partitions contained 62, 62 and 63 chains. (PDF p. 4).
- Training proteins were excluded if any domain shared a CATH fold with a test domain. Five PSI-BLAST iterations found no additional relationships across the sets. (PDF p. 4).
- The test set excluded the 16 architecture-selection targets and their homologs. Evaluation reported Q3 and Sov3, with two mappings from DSSP states. (PDF p. 4).
- CASP3 supplied an independent blind test. Assessors also evaluated a subset of difficult targets that methods predicted poorly on average. (PDF p. 5).

## Results

- Mean per-chain Q3 was 76.0%, with standard deviation 7.8%. Mean Sov3 was 73.5%, with standard deviation 12.7%. (PDF p. 4).
- On the difficult CASP3 targets, PSIPRED achieved Q3 of 73.4% and Sov3 of 71.9%. The next best method achieved 69.0% and 65.7%, respectively. (PDF p. 5).
- For the 21 submitted CASP3 targets in Table 1, residue-weighted Q3 was 75.7% under the main DSSP mapping and 77.6% under the simpler mapping. (PDF p. 6).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- PSI-BLAST can add repetitive sequences to a profile by mistake. Subsequent iterations can then report unrelated sequences with apparently high confidence. (PDF p. 2).
- The architecture search tested only a few alternatives and used a limited set of CASP2 targets. (PDF p. 3).
- The author notes that CASP has small samples. Architecture and training choices can also bias conventional cross-validation results. (PDF p. 5).
- CASP3 PHD predictions came through JPRED rather than the PHD authors. The author notes that a more recent PHD implementation might perform better. (PDF p. 6).
