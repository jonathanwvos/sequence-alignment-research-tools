## Executive summary

- BIMSA implements BiWFA on the UPMEM processing-in-memory platform to reduce sequence-alignment data movement. (PDF p. 2).
- It supports optimal alignment of sequences up to 100,000 bases. (PDF p. 1).
- The evaluation reports substantial gains over earlier PIM implementations, with benefits that depend on workload and transfer costs. (PDF p. 8).

## Objectives

- The authors aim to accelerate alignment from short to very long sequences through BiWFA's linear memory requirement. (PDF p. 2).
- They characterize memory bottlenecks, optimize BiWFA for UPMEM, and compare it with CPU and PIM implementations. (PDF p. 2).

## Arguments

- The authors argue that BiWFA remains limited by data movement despite its O(s) memory use and O(ns) runtime. (PDF p. 2).
- Independent sequence-pair tasks suit UPMEM because they require no communication between processing units. (PDF p. 4).

## Analysis

- CPU profiling uses four Genome in a Bottle datasets. It measures memory stalls, last-level cache misses, load latency, and memory-level parallelism. (PDF p. 3).
- The CPU implementation shows poor scaling. The PacBio.CSS example gains approximately fourfold speed with 16 threads. (PDF p. 3).
- The authors link slower execution on long, noisy sequences to wavefronts that exceed WRAM capacity. Heterogeneous sequence lengths and error rates also cause load imbalance. (PDF p. 8).

## Methods

- BiWFA advances wavefronts from both sequence ends until they meet at a breakpoint on an optimal alignment path. (PDF p. 3).
- BIMSA assigns one or more independent sequence pairs to each DPU thread. (PDF p. 4).
- BIMSA replaces recursion with iterative breakpoint subdivision. It applies regular WFA when a subproblem's score permits its memory use to fit the allocated WRAM. (PDF p. 4).
- Partial optimal alignments are stored consecutively in MRAM to build the complete alignment. (PDF p. 4).

## Applications

- BIMSA processes batches of independent sequence-pair alignments. (PDF p. 4).
- Real-data tests cover Illumina, PacBio.CSS, and Nanopore workloads. (PDF p. 8).

## Optimization

- Iterative subdivision avoids the limited DPU stack and controls the memory required by WFA base cases. (PDF p. 4).
- Adaptive wavefront transfers start at 8 bytes and increase by powers of two. Growth stops at the allocated buffer size or the 2048-byte transfer limit. (PDF p. 6).
- Sequence reads are limited to 16 characters because accesses are sparse. Larger transfers are used for sequential CIGAR output. (PDF p. 6).
- BIMSA-Hybrid transfers selected alignments to the CPU. This substantially reduces runtime for the tested heterogeneous long-read datasets. (PDF p. 8).

## Evaluation and testing

- Eight simulated datasets combine lengths of 150, 1000, 10,000, and 100,000 bases with error rates of 5% and 10%. Timings are averaged over five runs. (PDF p. 6).
- Comparators are CPU WFA2lib, WFA-GPU, and AIM implementations of Needleman–Wunsch, WFA, and adaptive WFA. (PDF p. 6).
- PIM comparisons use 2500 DPUs. BIMSA uses 12 tasklets, and the CPU baseline uses 16 hardware threads. (PDF p. 6).
- Primary PIM measurements report kernel time with data already in device memory. A separate table includes transfer time, and UPMEM-v1B results are projections. (PDF p. 6).

## Results

- On synthetic datasets, reported speedups reach 22.24 times over AIM-WFA and 5.84 times over CPU BiWFA. Average gains are 11.95 and 2.83 times, respectively. (PDF p. 8).
- AIM-WFA cannot process the tested sequences of 10,000 bases or longer because of memory limits. WFA-GPU is up to 1.8 times faster than BIMSA on the 10,000-base tests. (PDF p. 6).
- For PacBio.CSS, BIMSA-Hybrid reduces runtime from 182.4 to 7.6 seconds. For Nanopore, it reduces runtime from 314.5 to 105.6 seconds. (PDF p. 8).
- The UPMEM-v1B projection gives speedups up to 9.56 times over CPU BiWFA, with a 4.7-fold average. These are projected results. (PDF p. 8).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- Large wavefronts require more WRAM–MRAM transfers. The authors report degradation beginning with the 10,000-base, 5%-error case. (PDF p. 8).
- Host transfer costs can remove kernel-level gains. For 150-base sequences at 5% error, BIMSA takes 0.2 seconds without transfers and 2.5 seconds with transfers. CPU WFA2lib takes 0.9 seconds. (PDF p. 8).
- WFA-GPU is 1.4 times faster than BIMSA-Hybrid on PacBio.CSS and 1.8 times faster on Nanopore. (PDF p. 8).
- CPU recovery waits for other DPU work and restarts the alignment. The authors state that production use still needs pipeline integration, testing, and a more mature PIM ecosystem. (PDF p. 9).
