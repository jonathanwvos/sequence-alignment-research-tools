## Executive summary

- The authors present a computer method to find a maximum match between two protein sequences. It permits gaps and preserves sequence order. (PDF p. 1).
- The method represents residue pairs as matrix cells and sequence comparisons as paths. It finds the path with the largest sum of cell scores minus gap penalties. (PDF p. 2).
- The authors compare real protein scores with scores from shuffled sequences to assess statistical significance. (PDF p. 6).

## Objectives

- The method aims to detect homology and describe its nature when evidence supports it. (PDF p. 9).
- The method aims to exclude comparisons that cannot contribute to the maximum match. (PDF p. 1).

## Arguments

- The authors argue that direct sequence comparison without gaps cannot establish the full genetic relationship between proteins. (PDF p. 1).
- The authors warn that sequence similarity does not always imply common ancestry. They propose structural convergence as one possible cause of similarity. (PDF p. 9).
- The authors argue that no single set of cell scores and gap penalties is best for all protein pairs. Different evolutionary histories can require different values. (PDF p. 10).

## Analysis

- A valid path uses each residue at most once. Both matrix indices increase along the path, which preserves residue order. (PDF p. 2).
- The authors state that every partial or unnecessary path lies within at least one necessary path. Necessary paths start on the first row or column and reach the last row or column. (PDF p. 2).
- The authors interpret the improved significance from type 2 pair weights in hemoglobin–myoglobin comparisons as evidence of substantial evolutionary divergence. They also infer that few insertions or deletions occurred. (PDF p. 10).

## Methods

- The simplest scoring scheme assigns one to identical residue pairs and zero to other pairs. More general scores can use protein composition, codons, or neighboring cells. (PDF p. 2).
- A gap penalty is subtracted from the path score. The penalty can depend on gap size or direction. (PDF p. 2).
- Successive summation proceeds from sequence ends toward their starts. Each cell receives its own score plus the largest score among permitted successor cells. (PDF p. 2).
- The implementation builds the matrix row by row. Residue codes identify a pair type, and a variable set supplies the score for that type. (PDF p. 6).

## Applications

- The experiments compare hemoglobin with myoglobin and ribonuclease with lysozyme. (PDF p. 6).
- The authors propose extending the method to several proteins with an n-dimensional array. Each cell would contain one residue from each protein. (PDF p. 2).
- The authors state that the method can supply replacement counts or minimum mutation counts for estimates of evolutionary distance. (PDF p. 10).

## Optimization

- The search evaluates necessary paths instead of every possible partial comparison. The authors use this restriction to reduce the search needed for the maximum match. (PDF p. 2).
- The authors propose high gap penalties when few insertions or deletions are expected. They propose lower penalties when gaps must connect homologous regions. (PDF p. 10).
- The authors propose greater weight for non-identical residue pairs as evolutionary divergence increases. (PDF p. 10).

## Evaluation and testing

- For each variable set, the authors shuffle one protein sequence and calculate its maximum match ten times. They estimate the mean and standard deviation of these random scores. (PDF p. 6).
- The authors assess the real score by its distance from the random mean in standard deviation units. They assume that deviations above 3.0 represent less than 1% of the random population. (PDF p. 6).
- A pooled probit plot uses standardized random scores from the first six hemoglobin–myoglobin variable sets. The authors report a good fit and regard it as support for distribution estimates through two standard deviations. (PDF p. 6).
- Variable set 7 uses a penalty large enough to prevent gaps. The authors use it to represent a frame-shift comparison. (PDF p. 9).

## Results

- The illustrated matrix has a maximum match of eight. The figure shows alternative paths that attain this value. (PDF p. 3).
- All reported hemoglobin–myoglobin matches show significant deviations under the authors' criterion. (PDF p. 6).
- For ribonuclease–lysozyme, only variable set 3 shows a possible difference from chance. The authors leave its cause unresolved. (PDF p. 9).
- The authors report similar significance for hemoglobin–myoglobin variable sets 4, 6, and 7. They judge the small differences probably meaningless because of sampling and distribution errors. (PDF p. 9).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- Only ten shuffled comparisons were used per variable set to limit computer time. The authors warn that standard deviation estimates from this sample size can have large errors. (PDF p. 6).
- The statistical analysis assumes a normal distribution of random scores. The reported probit check supports estimates only through two standard deviations. (PDF p. 6).
- The significance of a sequence relationship depends on the selected cell scores and operation values. The authors identify no universally best settings. (PDF p. 10).
- In the authors' hypothetical model, continued evolutionary divergence eventually makes remaining homology undetectable by this method. (PDF p. 10).
