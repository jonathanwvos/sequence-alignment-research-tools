## Executive summary

- DEDAL combines contextual protein embeddings with Smith–Waterman local alignment. It learns substitution scores and affine gap penalties for each pair of residue positions. (PDF p. 2).
- The authors report improved alignment accuracy and homology detection, especially for sequences with low identity. (PDF p. 5).

## Objectives

- The authors aim to learn flexible alignment scores that adapt to each sequence pair and residue position. (PDF p. 2).

## Arguments

- The authors argue that fixed substitution scores and uniform gap penalties restrict alignment accuracy. No single substitution matrix works well for all protein pairs. (PDF p. 2).
- Joint training of the encoder and alignment parameterizer performed better than training the parameterizer with a fixed encoder. (PDF p. 6).

## Analysis

- Ablation tests found little performance change when gap penalties became position independent. Linear gap penalties caused a small decline for remote homologs. (PDF p. 6).
- Restricting substitution scores to amino acid identity caused a large performance decline. Results approached those of the best fixed substitution matrix. (PDF p. 6).
- The optimal alignment is piecewise constant in its scoring parameters. The authors therefore use smooth losses to train from alignment errors. (PDF p. 10).

## Methods

- The encoder has six transformer layers, 12 attention heads per layer, and 768-dimensional embeddings. Bilinear functions produce substitution scores. Softplus transforms produce nonnegative gap penalties. (PDF p. 9).
- Training combines masked language modeling on UniRef50, alignment learning from Pfam-A seed, and homology classification. Random flanks extend Pfam domains to create local alignment examples. (PDF p. 2).
- The final model uses a perturbation-based differentiable alignment method. Tests found no significant performance difference from the smoothing alternative. (PDF p. 6).
- The Fenchel–Young loss uses Gaussian perturbations of alignment parameters. A single perturbation supplies a stochastic gradient. The homology classifier corrects alignment scores for sequence length. (PDF p. 10).

## Applications

- DEDAL produces pairwise local protein alignments and scores sequence pairs for homology detection. (PDF p. 2).

## Optimization

- Training uses one million language-model pretraining steps and 1.3 million joint training steps. Adam uses an 8,000-step linear warm-up and inverse square-root learning-rate decay. (PDF p. 10).
- The homology loss is disabled during the first 300,000 joint training steps. (PDF p. 10).
- Standard alignment requires O(nm) time and space. The accelerator implementation uses a wavefront algorithm and batch parallelism. The authors report practical O(max(n,m)) execution time. (PDF p. 9).

## Evaluation and testing

- The benchmark separates unseen sequences from known families and sequences from unseen clans. Alignment quality uses F1 scores stratified by sequence identity. (PDF p. 3).
- The fixed-score baseline was selected from 1,428 combinations of BLOSUM, VTML, or PFASUM matrices and affine gap penalties. (PDF p. 11).
- Homology tests use AUROC and precision-recall area on raw and extended domains. Same-clan pairs from different families form a separate remote-homology group. (PDF p. 5).
- Homology results were averaged over ten replicates. Error bars show 95% confidence intervals. (PDF p. 6).

## Results

- In-distribution alignment F1 increased from 0.744 to 0.877. For identity at or below 10%, F1 increased from 0.152 to 0.587. (PDF p. 3).
- For extended domains below 10% identity, homology AUROC increased from 0.696 to 0.992 in distribution. It increased from 0.698 to 0.910 out of distribution. (PDF p. 5).
- Training took about one week on 32 TPU v3 cores. (PDF p. 3).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- Different Pfam clans serve as a proxy for nonhomology. The authors acknowledge that some unknown homologs can receive negative labels. (PDF p. 6).
- Performance declined on unseen clans. The authors interpret the larger decline for cross-family homologs as possible memorization of clan-specific motifs. (PDF p. 5).
- The main evaluation retains the UniRef50 language-model corpus across Pfam splits. Thus, unseen Pfam clans do not necessarily imply unseen language-model sequences. (PDF p. 3).

Sample size We analyze n=1,223,021 sequences from the Pfam-A seed database, which we split into training (80%), validation (5%) and test (15%) sets as explained in the manuscript. The sample size corresponds to >98% of the totality of Pfam-A seed database and was not otherwise optimized. It is sufficiently large to ensure that all performance measures we estimate are sufficiently accurate to draw statistically significant conclusions. For example, for the 'homology detection" tasks where we estimate AUROC of a binary classification problem to discriminate homologous pairs from non-homologous ones (Figure 4), the test set used to estimate AUROC has respectively >1 million and >5 million positive pairs, and the same amount of negative pairs; using the se_auc() method of R's auctestr package, we estimate that the standard error of the AUC estimate is <0.0005, which is more than two orders of magnitude smaller than the differences in AUROC between methods we study in Figure 4. Data exclusions We excluded from the analysis 24,151 sequences (about 2% of the total) with more than 512 amino acids, since the software used is optimized to process sequences of up to 512 amino acids. Replication Model training requires significant time and computation (several weeks on a large computer cluster), so we did not replicate the train/ validation/test set split as explained above. The DEDAL model itself is trained by stochastic gradient optimization, so for a given training set the model obtained is stochastic. We replicated the training of DEDAL ten times with a different random seed to study the fluctuations of the performance of the model due to the stochasticity of the training, and plot the corresponding error bars in all figures. Randomization For the construction of the training/validation/test sets from the set of all sequences in Pfam-A seed, which are organized by clans, we first randomly selected 255 and 955 clans to form the out-of-distribution validation (2.5%) and test (7.5%) sets, respectively. From the sequences in the remaining 10,493 clans, we selected uniformly at random the in-distribution validation (2.5%) and test (7.5%) sets, respectively. The remaining sequences form the training set (80%). Blinding Blinding is not relevant in this study, since there is no group allocation to assess a treatment effect.
