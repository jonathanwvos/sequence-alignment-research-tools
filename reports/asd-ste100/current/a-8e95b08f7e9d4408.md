## Executive summary

- AlphaGenome takes 1 Mb of DNA sequence and predicts 5,930 human or 1,128 mouse genome tracks across 11 modalities. (PDF p. 2).
- The authors report better performance than external models on 22 of 24 genome-track evaluations. The distilled model matches or exceeds external models on 25 of 26 variant-effect evaluations. (PDF p. 2).
- The model combines local convolutions, transformer blocks, and a U-Net-inspired architecture. Training uses pretraining followed by teacher-ensemble distillation. (PDF p. 2).

## Objectives

- Combine long DNA context, base-pair resolution, and multiple genomic modalities in one sequence-to-function model. (PDF p. 2).
- Evaluate predictions on unseen genome intervals and measure variant-effect prediction across several regulatory tasks. (PDF p. 2).

## Arguments

- The authors argue that short inputs can miss distal regulation. Coarse outputs can obscure splice sites and other fine-scale features. (PDF p. 1).
- The authors argue that specialized models alone cannot capture the full range of molecular effects across modalities. (PDF p. 2).

## Analysis

- Ablations vary target resolution, sequence length, ensemble size, distillation, and modality groups. Most comparisons use four training seeds and intervals of two standard deviations. (PDF p. 9).
- Single-modality comparisons restrict updates to shared representations to the selected modality group. These comparisons use eight seeds per group. (PDF p. 9).
- The eQTL analysis separates performance by tissue, variant-to-TSS distance, functional class, and indel size. It also compares random forests with different scorer features. (PDF p. 21).
- The enhancer–gene analysis tests input-gradient scores and variant-effect scores on ENCODE-rE2G. Logistic regression tests the added value of AlphaGenome features alongside existing features and TSS distance. (PDF p. 25).

## Methods

- The architecture produces one-dimensional embeddings at 1-bp and 128-bp resolution and pairwise embeddings at 2,048-bp resolution. Convolutions model local patterns; transformers model longer dependencies. (PDF p. 2).
- Most output heads apply linear transformations to embeddings. A separate splice-junction mechanism models interactions between donor and acceptor embeddings. (PDF p. 2).
- Pretraining uses experimental targets. Four-fold models reserve one quarter of the reference genome for validation and testing; all-fold models train on all available intervals. (PDF p. 2).
- A student learns predictions from frozen all-fold teachers. Distillation inputs include random shifts, reverse complements, and random mutations. (PDF p. 3).

## Applications

- The authors examine TAL1 oncogenic variants with multimodal predictions, shuffled controls, clustering, and in silico mutagenesis. Predicted expression increases are strongest in relevant haematopoietic contexts. (PDF p. 11).
- The authors propose hypothesis generation, rare-disease research, sequence design, and evaluation of generated DNA as future uses. (PDF p. 12).
- The released tools include an API, a Python SDK, quantile-calibrated variant scoring, and in silico mutagenesis contribution scores. (PDF p. 14).

## Optimization

- Sequence parallelism distributes the 1-Mb input across eight interconnected TPU v3 devices. This enables base-pair-resolution training. (PDF p. 2).
- The distilled student predicts across modalities with one device call per variant. The authors report a runtime below one second on an NVIDIA H100 GPU. (PDF p. 2).
- Distillation with many teachers produces single models competitive with some pretrained ensembles. Removing random input mutations reduces performance on several variant-effect tasks. (PDF p. 12).
- Full 1-Mb training and inference give the best overall results. Shorter inference inputs offer a speed option, with reduced performance. (PDF p. 12).

## Evaluation and testing

- Genome-track evaluation uses held-out regions and compares fold-specific models with the strongest available external model for each task. Metrics include correlations, auPRC, and profile Jensen–Shannon divergence. (PDF p. 3).
- The 26 variant-effect benchmarks cover expression, splicing, polyadenylation, enhancer–gene links, accessibility, and transcription-factor binding. These evaluations use the distilled student. (PDF p. 2).
- The CAGI5 saturation-mutagenesis MPRA evaluation compares AlphaGenome with Enformer, Borzoi, and ChromBPNet. LASSO combines prediction features across cell types and modalities. (PDF p. 11).
- All primary experimental training and evaluation data come from public sources. Detailed architecture, training, and benchmark protocols are deferred to supplementary information. (PDF p. 14).

## Results

- The authors report a 25.5% relative improvement over Borzoi for eQTL sign prediction and an 8.0% improvement over ChromBPNet for accessibility QTL prediction, averaged across five datasets. (PDF p. 3).
- On CAGI5, Pearson r increases from 0.57 for cell-type-matched DNase predictions to 0.63 with DNase features across cell types and 0.65 with multimodal features. (PDF p. 11).
- Base-pair targets give the best fine-scale track results. Coarser assay tracks and aggregated variant-effect metrics are less sensitive to target resolution. (PDF p. 11).
- The full multimodal model generally exceeds models trained on single modality groups. The benefit depends on the task, and eQTL prediction benefits from the full set of modalities. (PDF p. 12).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- Distal regulation beyond 100 kb, tissue-specific patterns, and condition-specific variant effects remain difficult to predict. (PDF p. 12).
- Training and evaluation focus heavily on protein-coding genes. Species coverage is limited to human and mouse, and evaluations mainly concern humans. (PDF p. 12).
- The authors have not benchmarked personal-genome prediction. The model predicts molecular consequences and does not directly cover the broader processes that determine complex traits. (PDF p. 12).
- High variant-score thresholds enrich causal candidates but give low recall, especially for GWAS variants. (PDF p. 11).
