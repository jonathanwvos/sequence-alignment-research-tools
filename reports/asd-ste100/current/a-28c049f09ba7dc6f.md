## Executive summary

- MMseqs2 supports protein sequence searches and iterative profile searches. It combines similar-k-mer filtering, vectorized ungapped alignment, and gapped Smith-Waterman alignment. (PDF p. 1).
- The authors report BLAST-level sensitivity at 36 times its speed. Three-iteration profile searches were more sensitive than PSI-BLAST and 433 times faster. (PDF p. 2).

## Objectives

- The authors aim to reduce the computational bottleneck in large metagenomic searches while retaining sensitivity to distant homologues. (PDF p. 1).

## Arguments

- The authors argue that consecutive similar 7-mer matches on one diagonal provide a selective filter. Homologues can preserve this pattern, but chance matches rarely do. (PDF p. 1).
- The authors link improved profile-search sensitivity partly to suppression of high-scoring false positives and more accurate E-values. (PDF p. 2).

## Analysis

- The authors attribute sensitivity comparable to SWIPE to suppression of matches between locally biased regions. This offsets some sensitivity loss from heuristic filters. (PDF p. 1).
- In 6370 searches, DIAMOND reported 69211 false positives with E-values below 0.001. MMseqs2 reported 54. These occurred in 5% and 0.1% of searches, respectively. (PDF p. 1).

## Methods

- A precomputed target index stores sequence identifiers and positions for each k-mer. Each spaced query k-mer generates a list of similar k-mers. Index lookups detect consecutive matches on the same diagonal. (PDF p. 2).
- Candidates pass through vectorized ungapped alignment and then gapped Smith-Waterman alignment. (PDF p. 1).
- Composition corrections, TANTAN masking in the k-mer index, and a small negative score offset reduce false matches and alignment overextension. (PDF p. 1).

## Applications

- The OM-RGC workflow searched eggNOG with increasing sensitivity. Only sequences without matches proceeded to the next search. (PDF p. 2).
- For remaining OM-RGC sequences, the authors built profiles using a combined UniProt and OM-RGC database clustered at 50% identity. They then searched eggNOG with these profiles. (PDF p. 2).
- The authors searched 1.1 billion predicted proteins against 16479 Pfam profiles. The search produced 370 million domain annotations with E-values below 0.001. (PDF p. 2).

## Optimization

- The k-mer similarity threshold controls the speed-sensitivity trade-off. The method generates about 600 to 60000 similar k-mers per query k-mer. (PDF p. 1).
- The innermost loop avoids random memory access. The authors report almost inverse runtime scaling with core count. (PDF p. 1).
- MMseqs2 combines manual vectorization, distribution of queries across cores, and distribution of target database chunks across servers. (PDF p. 1).

## Evaluation and testing

- The benchmark used 6370 queries, 3.4 million UniProt database sequences, and 27 million reversed sequences. Query regions without annotations were scrambled while preserving local amino acid composition. (PDF p. 1).
- Matches within a SCOP family were true positives. Matches to reversed sequences or different SCOP folds were false positives. Other cases were ignored. (PDF p. 1).
- Per-query sensitivity was the fraction of true positives ranked ahead of the first false positive by E-value. The authors called this AUC sensitivity. (PDF p. 1).
- Timing used a server with two eight-core processors and 128 GB RAM. The query set was duplicated 100 times. Database indexing time was excluded. (PDF p. 3).

## Results

- MMseqs2-sensitive matched BLAST sensitivity at 36 times its speed. (PDF p. 1).
- Three MMseqs2 sequence searches matched 69% of OM-RGC sequences in 1520 CPU hours. BLAST matched 67% in 162952 CPU hours. (PDF p. 2).
- Additional profile searches raised OM-RGC coverage to 78%. Total CPU time was 1.5% of the BLAST search time. (PDF p. 2).
- On 1100 sampled proteins, MMseqs2 at sensitivity 5 found 415 annotations versus 514 for HMMER3, at almost 14000 times its speed. Sensitivity 7 found 474 annotations at 4000 times its speed. (PDF p. 2).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- Database storage requires 13.4 GB plus 7 bytes per amino acid. The example database of 30.3 million sequences required about 80 GB. (PDF p. 1).
- Reported benchmark speedups exclude indexing. Indexing 30.3 million sequences took 11 minutes. (PDF p. 3).
- The Pfam sample shows a sensitivity cost: both tested MMseqs2 settings produced fewer annotations than HMMER3. (PDF p. 2).
