## Executive summary

- The authors examine how to interpret AlphaFold regions with low confidence that overlap intrinsically disordered regions. (PDF p. 1).
- They argue that an IDR requires a description of its conformational ensemble. A single static prediction cannot represent that ensemble. (PDF p. 3).

## Objectives

- The authors aim to prevent incorrect structural interpretations of IDRs and explain how quantitative sequence–ensemble relationships can support functional analysis. (PDF p. 2).

## Arguments

- The authors argue that most regions with very low confidence are likely disordered. They need not be folded structures that AlphaFold failed to predict. (PDF p. 3).
- IDR ensembles depend on sequence, solution conditions, and functional context. Static ribbon displays cannot establish ensemble dimensions or capture radius. (PDF p. 3).

## Analysis

- AlphaFold uses evolutionary covariation from large multiple sequence alignments. The authors identify high IDR variability across orthologs as a barrier to finding evolutionary constraints from alignments alone. (PDF p. 2).
- The authors describe pLDDT as a per-residue confidence score from 0 to 100. They report that roughly 30% of human proteome residues have scores below 50. (PDF p. 3).
- The authors favor inter-residue distance distributions and their moments as quantitative descriptions of conformational ensembles. (PDF p. 3).

## Methods

- The examples use atomistic simulations with the ABSINTH implicit solvation model. Experimental data validate the ensembles or provide restraints for refinement and reweighting. (PDF p. 6).
- For Httex1, large-scale Monte Carlo simulations provide initial ensembles. A Bayesian method reweights them with single-molecule FRET measurements collected at low picomolar concentrations. (PDF p. 7).
- For A1-LCD, integrated SAXS, NMR, and simulation results support a coarse-grained stickers-and-spacers model of phase behavior. (PDF p. 7).

## Applications

- The authors propose using validated ensembles and improved computational methods to study IDRs attached to folded domains. (PDF p. 6).
- A1-LCD ensembles help identify aromatic contacts that contribute to single-chain compaction and phase separation. (PDF p. 7).

## Optimization

Not stated in the extractable source pages.

## Evaluation and testing

- The p27 C-terminal ensemble was assessed with SAXS, NMR, paramagnetic relaxation measurements, and ion mobility mass spectrometry. (PDF p. 6).
- For A1-LCD, the authors compare the radius of gyration from the AlphaFold structure with direct SAXS measurements. (PDF p. 7).
- The authors cite CASP14 as an independent assessment of AlphaFold structure prediction. The excerpt does not provide detailed benchmark scores. (PDF p. 1).

## Results

- The p27 C-terminal ensemble has average properties consistent with Flory random coils. Experiments and validated simulations indicate a broad range of sizes and shapes. (PDF p. 6).
- Changes in p27 charge patterning change mean ensemble size monotonically. T187 phosphorylation efficiency does not follow the same monotonic relation. (PDF p. 6).
- The AlphaFold A1-LCD structure gives a larger radius of gyration than SAXS measurements. Integrated evidence indicates both compact and expanded conformations. (PDF p. 7).
- Reweighted Httex1 ensembles favor tadpole-like conformations. The polyQ region forms a compact head, and the Pro-rich region forms a semi-flexible tail. (PDF p. 7).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- A pLDDT threshold below 50 can overestimate disorder. Some low-confidence regions can fold when they bind, and the resulting structure can depend on the binding partner. (PDF p. 3).
- IDRs studied as isolated proteins can change their ensemble properties when attached to folded domains. (PDF p. 6).
- Self-association makes monomeric Httex1 difficult to characterize. Mixtures of oligomers and larger assemblies can distort structural measurements. (PDF p. 7).
