## Executive summary

- Myers relates longest common subsequences and shortest insertion-deletion scripts to paths in an edit graph. (PDF p. 1).
- The algorithm takes O(ND) time, where N is total input length and D is minimum edit-script length. It is intended for inputs with few differences. (PDF p. 1).
- The author presents a linear-space refinement and a suffix-tree variation with O(N log N + D²) worst-case time. (PDF p. 1).

## Objectives

- The author seeks a simple algorithm whose cost depends on the number of differences between the inputs. (PDF p. 2).
- The refinements aim to reduce storage and improve expected or worst-case runtime. (PDF p. 2).

## Arguments

- The author argues that D is often small in file comparison and related DNA comparisons. Match-pair counts can still be large for repetitive inputs or small alphabets. (PDF p. 2).
- A shortest-path view explains the connection between LCS and edit scripts. A specialized Dijkstra algorithm has the same time order but requires more complex queue management. (PDF p. 8).

## Analysis

- For sequence lengths M and N, relevant paths lie within diagonals −D to D. The stated bound on traversed diagonal edges gives O((M + N)D) time. (PDF p. 8).
- Snake traversal is the limiting operation. The remaining work takes O(D²) time. (PDF p. 8).
- The abstract states O(N + D²) expected time under a basic stochastic model, with N denoting total input length. (PDF p. 1).
- The linear-space refinement uses two shared O(D) vectors and O(log D) recursion storage. Retaining the input sequences gives O(M + N) total space. (PDF p. 13).

## Methods

- The edit graph has horizontal and vertical edges for edits. Diagonal edges exist where input symbols match. (PDF p. 2).
- The greedy search retains the current furthest-reaching endpoints in vector V. These endpoints suffice to report the optimal length. (PDF p. 8).
- To reconstruct a path, the basic method saves V after each edit-depth iteration and traces predecessor endpoints and maximal snakes. (PDF p. 8).
- The suffix-tree variation obtains maximal snake lengths through lowest-common-ancestor queries on suffixes of the combined inputs. (PDF p. 13).

## Applications

- The basic algorithm was used in a UNIX diff implementation. (PDF p. 2).
- The author identifies genetic evolution studies and comparisons of similar DNA sequences as applications of sequence differencing. (PDF p. 2).

## Optimization

- A threshold MAX limits the search to O((M + N)MAX) time when only bounded differences are of interest. (PDF p. 8).
- The middle-snake procedure completes before recursive calls. Its two vectors can therefore be shared across all calls. (PDF p. 13).
- Suffix-tree preprocessing replaces snake scans with O(1) queries. This yields O((M + N) log(M + N) + D²) time. (PDF p. 14).

## Evaluation and testing

- The author compares the diff implementation with System 5 diff, which uses Hunt and Szymanski's algorithm. (PDF p. 2).
- A reported large comparison used two 1.5-million-byte sequences on a VAX 785 running 4.2BSD UNIX. (PDF p. 2).

## Results

- The author reports that the new diff implementation usually runs two to four times faster than System 5 diff. (PDF p. 2).
- The linear-space refinement is reported to be about twice as slow as the basic algorithm. (PDF p. 2).
- The two 1.5-million-byte sequences were compared in less than two minutes, with more than 500 differences. (PDF p. 2).
- Saving endpoint vectors for traceback requires O(D²) space. Listing the resulting optimal path takes O(M + N) additional time. (PDF p. 8).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- Hunt and Szymanski's algorithm can be faster when D is large. The author gives completely different files as an example. (PDF p. 2).
- The expected-time bound depends on a stochastic model. It is not presented as an unconditional runtime guarantee. (PDF p. 1).
- The suffix-tree variation is mainly theoretical. The author identifies large constant costs and substantial auxiliary storage as practical obstacles. (PDF p. 14).
