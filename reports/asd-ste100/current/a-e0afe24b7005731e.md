## Executive summary

- The authors propose ant colony optimization (ACO) to find approximate longest common subsequences for multiple biological sequences. (PDF p. 1).
- ACO found longer common subsequences than ExpA and BNMAS in the reported DNA and protein tests. BNMAS used less CPU time. (PDF p. 17).

## Objectives

- The objective is to obtain good approximate solutions efficiently for multiple long biological sequences. (PDF p. 2).

## Arguments

- The authors motivate heuristic search through the NP-hardness of the general problem and the O(l^n) cost of dynamic programming for n sequences of length l. (PDF p. 2).
- They argue that approximation ratios equal to the alphabet size give weak practical guarantees for DNA and proteins. (PDF p. 2).

## Analysis

- The objective maximizes subsequence length while preserving character order in every input sequence. (PDF p. 3).
- The authors compare solution lengths through percentage gains relative to ExpA and BNMAS. These gains compare algorithms rather than establish distance from an optimum. (PDF p. 15).

## Methods

- ACO maintains pheromone values on characters in a subset R of the input sequences. The size of R equals the number of ants and is smaller than the input set. (PDF p. 18).
- The heuristic favors states with fewer unselected characters within a fixed window. State transitions use exploitation, biased exploration, and exclusion within that window. (PDF p. 18).
- Local search inserts additional characters into the best solution of a generation without reducing its existing content. (PDF p. 18).

## Applications

- The authors describe LCS as a basis for multiple sequence alignment, similarity-based gene prediction, and genome rearrangement analysis. (PDF p. 2).

## Optimization

- Window-limited state selection restricts the search to a local region. The authors identify window size as a factor in computational efficiency. (PDF p. 18).
- The authors report extensive parameter testing. Suitable window sizes tended to increase with alphabet size. (PDF p. 18).

## Evaluation and testing

- DNA tests used random sequences and rat and virus sequences from NCBI. Each sequence had 600 characters. Test sets contained 10 to 200 sequences. (PDF p. 14).
- Protein tests used the same sequence lengths and set sizes, with random, rat, and virus data. (PDF p. 15).
- A larger test used separate sets of 300 primate DNA and protein sequences from NCBI. Each sequence had 600 characters. (PDF p. 17).
- The comparisons measured common subsequence length and CPU time. BNMAS and ACO results were averages of 10 independent runs. (PDF p. 14).

## Results

- For 200 rat DNA sequences, ACO obtained mean length 114.6 in 65.5 seconds. BNMAS obtained 107.2 in 16.4 seconds. ExpA obtained 103 in 1866.7 seconds. (PDF p. 14).
- For 300 primate protein sequences, ACO obtained mean length 28.2 in 45.6 seconds. BNMAS obtained 24.1 in 37.1 seconds. ExpA obtained 19 in 8438.7 seconds. (PDF p. 17).
- The authors report larger solution-quality gains for proteins than for DNA. (PDF p. 17).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- ACO required more CPU time than BNMAS in the reported comparisons. (PDF p. 17).
- The authors leave the relationship between alphabet size and suitable window size for further study. (PDF p. 18).
- All algorithms discussed report only one common subsequence, although an input set can have multiple longest common subsequences. (PDF p. 3).
