## Executive summary

- The authors combine Burrows–Wheeler compression with suffix arrays to support searches in compressed text. (PDF p. 2).
- For text length U and pattern length p, the index reports occ occurrences in O(p + occ log^ε U) time for any fixed ε > 0. Storage is O(H_k(T)) + o(1) bits per symbol for fixed k. (PDF p. 2).
- The paper extends the index to dynamic text collections and proposes its use in Compressed Glimpse. (PDF p. 2).

## Objectives

- Reduce index storage according to text compressibility while retaining fast searches for arbitrary patterns. (PDF p. 2).
- Support insertion, deletion, and pattern searches in a compressed collection of texts. (PDF p. 7).

## Arguments

- The authors argue that scanning the whole compressed text remains unsuitable for large collections, even when compression speeds up the scan. (PDF p. 2).
- The transformed text implicitly contains suffix-array information. Auxiliary structures can expose this information without full decompression at query time. (PDF p. 3).

## Analysis

- The analysis assumes a constant-size text alphabet. It relates storage to kth-order empirical entropy. (PDF p. 3).
- The dynamic structure searches O(log^3 U) compressed subsets. Theorem 4 gives O(p log^3 U + occ log U) query time, O(t log U) amortized insertion time, and O(t log^2 U) amortized deletion time. (PDF p. 8).
- The CGlimpse analysis assumes Heaps law, generalized Zipf law, and a sublinear bound on approximate word matches. Under these assumptions, space overhead and query time are sublinear for any block size. (PDF p. 8).

## Methods

- The Burrows–Wheeler transform sorts cyclic shifts of the text with an end marker and takes their last column. LF-mapping links this column to the sorted first column. (PDF p. 3).
- The basic location method marks rows at sampled text positions. It follows LF-mapping until it reaches a marked row with a stored suffix position. (PDF p. 6).
- The dynamic method groups texts into subsets by total length. Insertions merge subsets and rebuild their compressed indexes. Deletions use logical marks followed by physical reconstruction. (PDF p. 7).

## Applications

- CGlimpse indexes each text block with the compressed index. Queries search candidate block indexes instead of scanning each complete block. (PDF p. 8).

## Optimization

- Two-level buckets and Packed B-trees support constant-time tests for marked rows. The basic scheme uses O(U/log U) extra bits and O(log^2 U) time per reported position. (PDF p. 6).
- A hierarchy of grouped text blocks provides LF-mapping shortcuts. Repeated shortcuts reduce location time to O(log^ε U) per occurrence for fixed ε > 0. (PDF p. 6).

## Evaluation and testing

- The paper compares asymptotic storage and query bounds with suffix trees, suffix arrays, and succinct suffix arrays. (PDF p. 2).
- CGlimpse is evaluated through a theoretical information-retrieval model. The reported 30–50% performance improvement concerns earlier compressed-block methods. (PDF p. 8).

## Results

- The authors obtain entropy-dependent index storage with O(p) pattern search and O(log^ε U) location time per occurrence. (PDF p. 2).
- Under the stated retrieval model, CGlimpse removes the block-size restrictions needed for classical Glimpse to achieve both space and time sublinearity. (PDF p. 8).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- Dynamic text identifiers require O(m log U) extra bits for m texts. This overhead is sublinear when individual texts have length ω(log U) bits. (PDF p. 8).
- The authors leave O(occ/B) external-memory reporting and general O(occ) occurrence retrieval as open goals. (PDF p. 8).
- The supplied excerpt defers the LF-mapping lemma details and proof to the full paper. (PDF p. 6).
