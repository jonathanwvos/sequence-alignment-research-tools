## Executive summary

- Gotoh develops four dynamic programming algorithms for aligning two prealigned sequence groups with internal gaps. (PDF p. 1).
- Algorithm D exactly optimizes the group-to-group sum-of-pairs score with affine gap costs. Its typical computation cost is close to O(MNL^2). (PDF p. 1).
- The algorithms support progressive alignment and randomized group realignment for multiple sequence alignment. (PDF p. 6).

## Objectives

- Account for pre-existing gaps when calculating gap-opening costs between sequence groups. (PDF p. 1).
- Obtain exact group-to-group score optimization at a practical computation cost. (PDF p. 1).

## Arguments

- Gotoh argues that internal gap locations contain useful information for aligning distantly related sequences. (PDF p. 1).
- New group-level gaps leave within-group pair scores unchanged. Thus, optimizing the total score reduces to optimizing the cross-group score. (PDF p. 3).
- Exact group realignment cannot worsen the total sum-of-pairs score. This property supports convergence of randomized refinement. (PDF p. 6).

## Analysis

- Algorithm A applies conventional dynamic programming with a uniform gap-opening penalty. It does not account for the positions of internal gaps. (PDF p. 4).
- Algorithm B estimates ambiguous gap openings. Profile preprocessing permits O(L^2) computation. (PDF p. 4).
- Algorithm C tracks consecutive null counts to detect gap openings. It takes O(MNL^2) time and O((M + N) min(I,J)) space, excluding possible traceback storage. (PDF p. 4).
- Algorithm D retains multiple candidates and removes candidates through four tests. Its typical cost is close to O(MNL^2), but its worst-case cost is unknown. (PDF p. 6).

## Methods

- Each prealigned column acts as one unit. Pair scores combine residue dissimilarities, gap-extension costs, and gap-opening costs; matching nulls contribute zero. (PDF p. 2).
- Randomized refinement divides the current alignment into two groups, removes columns containing only nulls, and realigns the groups. (PDF p. 6).
- The C implementations provide algorithms A–D in alp/aln and randomized refinement in rrp/rrn. (PDF p. 6).

## Applications

- The methods align protein families through a given binary tree or refine an existing multiple alignment through randomized grouping. (PDF p. 6).
- Refinement with algorithm D improved the ten-globin alignment by 1941 score units and brought the heme-binding histidines into reasonable alignment. (PDF p. 9).

## Optimization

- Algorithm D tests criterion 4 first because it is the least costly test. Nested tests reduce large candidate lists; the default threshold is M + N. (PDF p. 6).
- Algorithm D nominated 3.2 ± 0.3 candidates and retained 1.4 ± 0.1 candidates per node on average. Algorithm C uses fixed counts of seven and three. (PDF p. 7).
- Algorithm B uses a default gap-opening estimation parameter of 0.65. Test runs of algorithm C gave estimates between 0.6 and 0.7. (PDF p. 7).

## Evaluation and testing

- Progressive tests used five serine proteases, ten non-vertebrate globins, eight GalE-related enzymes, and ten bacterial-type cytochrome P450 sequences. (PDF p. 6).
- Tests used negative MDM78 dissimilarities, gap-extension cost u = 6, and gap-opening cost v = 6. (PDF p. 3).
- Table I compares CPU time and sum-of-pairs scores in a fixed progressive order. CPU times were measured on a SONY NEWS 830. (PDF p. 7).
- Randomized refinement tests used five serine proteases and six insect globins. Each condition had 32 runs, with either gapless or progressive starting alignments. (PDF p. 8).

## Results

- Algorithms C and D produced equal progressive scores except for the GalE family, where D gave the lower score. C and D matched B on serine proteases and improved on A and B for the other families. (PDF p. 7).
- Algorithm A was about five times faster than the other algorithms. Algorithm D required about 10–20% more CPU time than C. (PDF p. 6).
- Original randomized refinement showed score increases with A and B, and sometimes C. No score increase occurred with D. (PDF p. 7).
- Progressive starting alignments generally reduced refinement steps and improved final scores. More rigorous group-alignment algorithms generally improved refinement results. (PDF p. 7).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- Algorithm C can discard a candidate needed for the optimal alignment. The example in Figure 2 gives a score two units worse than D. (PDF p. 4).
- Algorithms C and D require computation proportional to the product of group sizes. Their gap rules are difficult to combine with profile operations. (PDF p. 9).
- Exact group-to-group optimization does not establish a global optimum for the full multiple alignment. This is an interpretation of the fixed-group optimization and iterative procedure. (PDF p. 6).
- The tested best scores are the best found through iterative refinement, rather than independently established global optima. (PDF p. 7).
