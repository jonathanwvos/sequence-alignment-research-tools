## Executive summary

- The authors propose an LCS algorithm that uses ordered match pairs for two DNA sequences. (PDF p. 1).
- The algorithm builds chains of match pairs and selects a longest chain. (PDF p. 3).
- The reported complexity bounds are inconsistent across the implementation analysis and conclusion. (PDF p. 3).

## Objectives

- The authors aim to reduce the time and space needed to find an LCS. (PDF p. 1).

## Arguments

- The authors connect LCS to minimum edit distance when substitutions are excluded. (PDF p. 1).
- Their correctness argument states that consecutive pairs in a longest chain must be direct successors. Otherwise, another match pair could extend the chain. (PDF p. 3).

## Analysis

- Algorithm 1 uses a dynamic programming table. The authors assign it O(mn) time and O(mn) space. (PDF p. 2).
- For m=O(n), the implementation analysis assigns O(n+l) time and space to match-pair construction. Here, l is the number of matches. (PDF p. 3).
- The same analysis assigns O(lL) time and O(l+L) space to chain construction. Here, L is the LCS length. (PDF p. 3).

## Methods

- A match pair contains positions with equal symbols. One pair precedes another when both position coordinates are smaller. (PDF p. 2).
- Algorithm 2 constructs match pairs in the order of the first sequence. It then constructs chains through the direct-successor relation. (PDF p. 2).
- The implementation records the occurrence positions of each nucleotide in a table for the second sequence. (PDF p. 3).

## Applications

- The method targets DNA sequence comparison over the alphabet A, C, G, T. (PDF p. 1).
- The authors state that the implementation also applies to general sequences with an alphabet of size p. (PDF p. 3).

## Optimization

- The algorithm omits separately listed chains whose starting pair has a direct predecessor. The authors argue that these chains are contained in predecessor chains. (PDF p. 3).
- The nucleotide occurrence table takes O(n) time and space to construct and supports match-pair generation. (PDF p. 3).

## Evaluation and testing

- The worked example compares ATCTGAT with TGCATA. It lists 12 match pairs and seven candidate chains. (PDF p. 3).

## Results

- The example has three longest listed chains, each with four match pairs. The authors show an alignment from one of these chains. (PDF p. 3).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- The introduction claims O(nL) time and O(n) space for the implementation. (PDF p. 1).
- The later analysis includes O(lL) chain construction, but the conclusion claims O(n+l) time and space. Interpretation: these statements do not establish a consistent total complexity bound. (PDF p. 3).
- The suffix-based explanation advances to later suffixes, but Algorithm 1 uses preceding indices. Interpretation: the indexing does not consistently express the stated suffix formulation. (PDF p. 2).
