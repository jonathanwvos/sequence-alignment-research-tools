## Executive summary

- Profile analysis detects distant protein relationships with a position-specific scoring matrix built from aligned family sequences. The matrix includes amino acid scores and gap penalties. (PDF p. 1).
- The authors report complete score separation of globins from nonglobins in the tested database. The immunoglobulin profile also detected related T-cell receptor sequences. (PDF p. 3).

## Objectives

- The authors aim to detect distantly related proteins by combining family sequence information with structural information. (PDF p. 1).

## Arguments

- The authors argue that one sequence cannot represent all sequence variation compatible with a protein fold. An aligned family provides more information for recognition. (PDF p. 1).
- The authors attribute selectivity to position-specific amino acid preferences and gap penalties. Dynamic programming places gaps under these constraints. (PDF p. 4).

## Analysis

- The authors describe templates as special cases of profiles. Profiles can score residues absent from the probe and permit gaps within conserved segments when the alignment score improves sufficiently. (PDF p. 4).
- The displayed consensus selects the residue with the highest profile score at each position. It need not select the most frequent probe residue. (PDF p. 3).

## Methods

- PROFMAKE builds an N-by-21 matrix from aligned probe sequences. Twenty columns score amino acids through weighted sums of Dayhoff matrix values. The final column specifies gap penalties. The reported examples use simple frequency weights. (PDF p. 1).
- The gap cost is PEN′ × [OPEN + EXN × L], where L is gap length. The examples use OPEN = 5 and EXN = 0.1. Position-specific penalties allow structural regions to have different gap costs. (PDF p. 2).
- PROFANAL reads replacement scores from the profile row and target-residue column. Single-sequence comparisons generally use Smith–Waterman local alignment. Database searches and dot plots use the forward/backward matrix method. (PDF p. 3).

## Applications

- The method tests whether a protein sequence belongs to a known family. (PDF p. 4).
- The authors propose libraries of family profiles to infer structural motifs. They also propose profile scores based on hydrophobicity, secondary structure preference, or side-chain volume. (PDF p. 4).

## Optimization

- For database searches and dot plots, the implementation omits alignment traceback. It retains only the highest score or scores above a specified threshold. (PDF p. 3).

## Evaluation and testing

- The authors searched PIR Release 8.0, which contained 3800 sequences. Tests ran on a VAX 11/780 with 16 MB of memory and a floating-point accelerator. (PDF p. 3).
- The globin profile used five structurally aligned representative sequences and contained 124 positions. The immunoglobulin profile used four variable-region segments and contained 49 positions including gaps. (PDF p. 3).
- The comparison used FASTP with a human alpha-hemoglobin probe and a 45-residue immunoglobulin heavy-chain probe. (PDF p. 3).

## Results

- Globin scores ranged from 469.5 to 928.7. Nonglobin scores ranged from 32.4 to 455.8. FASTP selected 244 of 271 globins for score optimization but did not clearly separate leghemoglobins from nonglobins. (PDF p. 3).
- Immunoglobulin variable-region scores ranged from 221.9 to 386.9. Nonimmunoglobulin scores ranged from 21.7 to 220.7 when T-cell receptors and immunoglobulin constant regions were excluded. (PDF p. 3).
- A 45-residue profile required approximately 20 CPU minutes to search the 3800-sequence PIR database. (PDF p. 3).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- Interpretation: The reported tests cover two protein families in one database. They do not establish equivalent selectivity across other families. (PDF p. 3).
- The authors present structural motif inference as a possible future use. The reported experiments test family detection. (PDF p. 4).
