## Executive summary

- The authors present merged longest common subsequence algorithms for similar sequences. The merge preserves the order of characters taken from each input sequence. (PDF p. 2).
- The algorithms address both unrestricted merging and merging with block constraints. Their runtime depends on the difference between target length and answer length. (PDF p. 1).

## Objectives

- The study aims to improve MLCS and BMLCS computation when the merged sequence and target are highly similar. (PDF p. 3).

## Arguments

- The authors contrast their goal with earlier algorithms that perform well when the answer length is small. They use the diagonal concept from an earlier LCS algorithm to address similar inputs. (PDF p. 3).

## Analysis

- Let m and n be the shorter and longer input lengths, r the target length, L the answer length, and |Σ| the alphabet size. MLCS takes O(n|Σ| + (r − L + 1)Lm) time and O(n|Σ| + Lm) space. (PDF p. 1).
- For BMLCS, let δ be the larger input block count. The bounds are O(n|Σ| + (r − L + 1)Lδ) time and O(n|Σ| + Lδ) space. (PDF p. 1).
- The authors correct an earlier MLCS recurrence. The condition for considering skip transitions uses mismatch in either input, rather than mismatch in both inputs. (PDF p. 3).

## Methods

- MLCS finds the longest target subsequence obtainable by interleaving subsequences of A and B while preserving their individual orders. (PDF p. 2).
- BMLCS adds block constraints. Characters selected from one block must appear together as a substring of the answer. (PDF p. 2).
- The proposed algorithms extend A and B as computation progresses along target T. (PDF p. 15).

## Applications

- The authors describe voice recognition from incomplete noisy streams as a possible application. (PDF p. 2).
- They also describe gene loss after genome duplication as an application, using two yeast species as an example. (PDF p. 2).

## Optimization

- The runtime includes the factor r − L + 1. This factor becomes small when the answer covers nearly all of the target. (PDF p. 1).
- The BMLCS algorithm benefits from fewer blocks. The authors identify bit parallelism as a possible future acceleration. (PDF p. 15).

## Evaluation and testing

- Experiments compared execution time on pseudorandom inputs and real DNA sequences. BMLCS tests included five and 10 blocks per input and varied alphabet size and similarity. (PDF p. 14).
- The DNA benchmark used 31 sequence sets from Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Kluyveromyces waltii. Targets came from predicted open reading frames, and input pairs came from gene correspondences. (PDF p. 14).
- DNA target lengths ranged from 15,237 to 56,451 bases. Reported similarities ranged from 88.35% to 100%. (PDF p. 14).

## Results

- On the real DNA benchmark, the proposed MLCS algorithm was faster than the bit-parallel comparator when similarity exceeded 96%. (PDF p. 14).
- The proposed BMLCS algorithm performed best on very similar inputs. The DP, S-table, and bit-parallel comparators changed little with similarity. (PDF p. 14).
- With alphabet size 1000, the proposed MLCS algorithm also won below 30% similarity. The authors attribute this result to the small answer length. (PDF p. 14).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- Performance can decline when T is much longer than A and B combined. The target length then dominates the runtime expression. (PDF p. 15).
- The supplied excerpts describe the algorithmic motivation and complexity, but omit the detailed proposed MLCS and BMLCS procedures. (PDF p. 3).
