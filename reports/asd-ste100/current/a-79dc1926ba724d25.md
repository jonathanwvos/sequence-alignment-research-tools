## Executive summary

- BLAT indexes nonoverlapping genome K-mers, finds candidate regions, aligns them, and joins the alignments. It includes specific handling for introns and missed internal exons. (PDF p. 2).
- The author reports fast mRNA-to-genome and translated vertebrate alignments. Performance depends on search settings and sequence similarity. (PDF p. 2).

## Objectives

- Align millions of ESTs and mouse genomic reads to the human genome within two weeks on a 90-CPU cluster. This supports frequent genome annotation updates. (PDF p. 2).
- Select search parameters that retain homologous regions while limiting unnecessary detailed alignments. (PDF p. 4).

## Arguments

- Indexing the genome lets BLAT scan the shorter query instead of scanning the complete database for each query. The author identifies this as the main source of speed. (PDF p. 10).
- Multiple nearby matches or longer matches with one mismatch can provide greater specificity at a given sensitivity than one perfect match. (PDF p. 10).

## Analysis

- With independent letters, the probability of detecting a homologous region with one perfect seed is P = 1 − (1 − M^K)^floor(H/K). M is identity and H is region length. (PDF p. 4).
- With equally likely letters, the expected chance-match count is F = (Q − K + 1)(G/K)(1/A)^K. Q is query length, G is database length, and A is alphabet size. (PDF p. 4).
- Hit sorting takes O(N log N) time in the stated analysis. HSP chaining uses O(N²) dynamic programming, where N is the HSP count. (PDF p. 8).

## Methods

- BLAT searches a genome index for perfect or near-perfect seed matches. It extends candidate regions and joins their alignments into larger gene-scale alignments. (PDF p. 2).
- Nucleotide alignment extends small exact matches, merges overlapping hits, and links hits in coordinate order. It recursively searches remaining gaps with smaller seeds. (PDF p. 8).
- Protein alignment extends seeds into ungapped HSPs with match score +2 and mismatch score −1. A weighted HSP graph and dynamic programming select the highest-scoring chain. (PDF p. 8).
- For mRNA, BLAT searches gaps between joined blocks for missed exons. It adjusts equivalent intron placements toward GT/AG splice boundaries. (PDF p. 8).

## Applications

- BLAT generated human EST and mRNA alignments and translated mouse-to-human genome alignments. It also supported interactive sequence searches through a client/server system. (PDF p. 2).

## Optimization

- The index uses four bytes per entry and stores nonoverlapping words. Batch processing builds it once per batch; the server retains it between queries. (PDF p. 10).
- Standalone BLAT splits nucleotide queries longer than 5,000 bases and translated queries longer than 1,500 bases. Subqueries overlap by about 250 bases and are joined after alignment. (PDF p. 8).
- The author evaluates K-mer size, mismatch allowance, and required hit count to balance speed and sensitivity. (PDF p. 2).

## Evaluation and testing

- The mRNA test remapped 713 annotated chromosome 22 transcripts with BLAT and Sim4. Only the highest-scoring BLAT alignment was retained for each transcript. (PDF p. 3).
- A translated timing test compared BLAT and WU-TBLASTX on 1,000 mouse reads against repeat-masked human chromosome 22. A larger test compared coding-base and exon coverage using 13 million mouse reads. (PDF p. 3).
- Parameter tests aligned 10,000 ESTs to three genomic target sizes. Another test compared translated search settings using 86,000 nonhuman mRNAs against the human genome. (PDF p. 9).

## Results

- BLAT agreed with chromosome 22 annotations at 99.99% of annotated bases and took 26 seconds. Sim4 agreed at 99.66% and took 17,468 seconds. (PDF p. 3).
- In the 1,000-read translated test, BLAT took 61 seconds with one 5-mer seed and 37 seconds with two 4-mer seeds. WU-TBLASTX took 2,736 and 2,714 seconds under the two tested matrices. (PDF p. 3).
- In the larger translated test, BLAT covered 80.8% of RefSeq coding bases and touched 86.7% of coding exons. WU-TBLASTX covered 81.7% of coding bases and touched 84.5% of coding exons. (PDF p. 3).
- The reported human EST run processed 3.73 million ESTs in 220 CPU hours. The translated mouse genome run took 16,300 CPU hours. (PDF p. 2).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- The nucleotide alignment strategy loses effectiveness below 90% identity. The author therefore considers it unsuitable for most cross-species nucleotide alignments. (PDF p. 8).
- Protein alignment can lose sequence around an indel unless the search finds seeds on both sides. Banded Smith–Waterman is proposed as a future replacement for the extension stage. (PDF p. 8).
- The author does not recommend queries longer than 200,000 bases and states that BLASTP is more sensitive for protein comparisons. (PDF p. 3).
- BLAT missed two small initial exons in the chromosome 22 test. Other disagreements involved splice placement, an ambiguous exon location, and degenerate sequence. (PDF p. 3).
