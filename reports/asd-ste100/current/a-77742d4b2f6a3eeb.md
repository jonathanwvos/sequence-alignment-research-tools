## Executive summary

- The authors compute an LCS alignment that first maximizes equal character pairs and then minimizes insertions and deletions. (PDF p. 1).
- Their run-length encoded algorithm outputs an optimum alignment in O(mN + nM) time and O(nm) working space. N and M are decoded lengths. n and m are run counts. (PDF p. 16).

## Objectives

- Select the shortest alignment among alignments with the maximum number of equal pairs. (PDF p. 2).
- Use run boundaries to reduce computation compared with the O(NM) cell-based dynamic program. (PDF p. 2).

## Arguments

- An LCS length alone does not distinguish alignments with different gap counts. The DNA example has two LCS choices, but only the AA choice permits a gap-free alignment. (PDF p. 1).
- Within each run-pair block, all diagonal steps have the same type. Thus, the optimum local score depends on displacement rather than individual cells. (PDF p. 2).

## Analysis

- The score s = (N + M + 1) × equ − ins − del enforces the objective. One extra equal pair outweighs any possible gap penalty difference. (PDF p. 3).
- A diagonal step is at least as good as a deletion followed by an insertion. An optimum local path therefore uses the maximum possible number of diagonal steps. (PDF p. 17).
- The authors derive a block transfer function with constantly many affine pieces. Boundary maximization takes time linear in the block perimeter. (PDF p. 17).

## Methods

- The baseline dynamic program takes the maximum of deletion, insertion, and diagonal scores. Gaps score −1, equal pairs score w, and replacements score zero. (PDF p. 3).
- The compressed algorithm represents block-boundary score arrays as sorted affine pieces. Each piece stores an integer interval, an offset, and a slope. (PDF p. 12).
- Hirschberg reconstruction computes forward and backward separator arrays. It scans their common refinement to select a crossing point with maximum combined score. (PDF p. 16).

## Applications

- The method applies to strings with long runs of equal characters, for which run-length encoding can reduce the input representation. (PDF p. 1).
- The algorithm can also output the LCS induced by the minimum-gap alignment within the stated time and space bounds. (PDF p. 16).

## Optimization

- Piecewise-affine arrays support restriction, coordinate shifts, coordinate reversal, and affine addition in linear time in the number of pieces. (PDF p. 12).
- A deque constructs the upper envelope of constants on ordered, non-nested intervals in O(K) time and space. (PDF p. 12).
- The reconstruction discards separator arrays before recursive calls and streams the alignment. This keeps working space within O(nm). (PDF p. 16).

## Evaluation and testing

- A worked example uses TTAACC and CCAAGG with w = 13. The gap-free AA alignment scores 26. The illustrated CC alignment has eight gaps and scores 18. (PDF p. 3).
- The complexity analysis uses the word-RAM model with word size at least proportional to log(N + M). Working space excludes input and output. (PDF p. 2).

## Results

- The baseline algorithm solves MinCostLCS in O(NM) time. Its scores fit in a constant number of machine words. (PDF p. 3).
- Theorem 5 gives O(mN + nM) time and O(nm) working space for nonempty RLE inputs, including alignment reconstruction. (PDF p. 16).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- Storing the complete alignment requires an additional Θ(N + M) space in the worst case. The stated working-space bound assumes streamed output. (PDF p. 16).
- The authors propose faster boundary data structures as future work. They do not establish the suggested improvement to the time bound. (PDF p. 17).
- Interpretation: the conclusion's question about compressed boundary storage is inconsistent with the piecewise-affine storage result already stated in Theorem 5. (PDF p. 17).
