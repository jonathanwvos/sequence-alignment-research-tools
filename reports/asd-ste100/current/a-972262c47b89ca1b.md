## Executive summary

- The authors derive BLOSUM substitution matrices from residue pairs in conserved, ungapped protein blocks. (PDF p. 1).
- They report improved alignment and homology-search performance relative to PAM matrices across the tests used. (PDF p. 5).

## Objectives

- The authors test whether observed relationships in protein blocks can improve detection of distant similarity compared with extrapolated mutation rates. (PDF p. 1).

## Arguments

- The authors argue that direct observations from conserved blocks can better support searches and alignments than rates extrapolated from closely related proteins. (PDF p. 5).
- The authors suggest that constraints in conserved regions can explain some BLOSUM–PAM differences. They present this as a possible explanation. (PDF p. 5).

## Analysis

- Relative entropy permits comparison of the matrix families. BLOSUM 62 and PAM 160 both have relative entropy of about 0.70 bits. (PDF p. 3).
- Compared with PAM 160, BLOSUM 62 is less tolerant of substitutions involving hydrophilic residues and more tolerant of those involving hydrophobic residues. (PDF p. 3).

## Methods

- The method counts all unordered residue pairs in each block column. A block with width w and s sequences contributes w × s × (s − 1) / 2 pairs. (PDF p. 1).
- Each score is log2 of the observed pair probability divided by its chance expectation. Scores are multiplied by two and rounded to integers. (PDF p. 1).
- Sequence segments are clustered by a specified identity threshold. Each cluster contributes the weight of one sequence, which reduces the effect of closely related members. (PDF p. 2).
- PROTOMAT and BLOSUM were applied iteratively. The final matrix family used 2,106 blocks from 559 Prosite groups. (PDF p. 2).

## Applications

- The matrices supply substitution scores for global multiple alignment, local pairwise alignment, and database homology searches. (PDF p. 2).
- BLOSUM 62 detected a suspected relationship between mariner and Tc1 transposon proteins that searches with PAM matrices had missed. (PDF p. 4).

## Optimization

- The clustering threshold controls the matrix family. At 62% clustering, approximately 1.25 million weighted pairs remain, compared with more than 15 million pairs without clustering. (PDF p. 2).
- Among the tested BLAST matrices, BLOSUM 62 performed slightly better overall than BLOSUM 60 or 70. (PDF p. 4).

## Evaluation and testing

- MULTALIN alignments of three serine proteases were checked against 161 positions aligned from known three-dimensional structures. (PDF p. 3).
- Search tests used Swiss-Prot 20 with 22,654 sequences and queries from 504 Prosite groups. The programs were BLASTP, FASTA, and SSEARCH. (PDF p. 2).
- Group sequences used for block construction were treated as positives. BLAST misses were unreported positives. FASTA and SSEARCH misses fell below the 99.5th percentile of negative scores. (PDF p. 2).
- RDF2 tested the transposon alignment against 1,000 sequence shuffles with a window size of 10. (PDF p. 4).

## Results

- MULTALIN with BLOSUM 62 missed 6 structural alignment positions. PAM 120, PAM 160, and PAM 250 missed 30–31 positions. (PDF p. 3).
- In BLAST searches, BLOSUM 62 outperformed PAM 140 for 90 groups and performed worse for 23 groups. (PDF p. 4).
- For receptor-family searches, BLOSUM matrices in the tested 0.3–0.8 entropy range missed 12–25 fewer members than PAM matrices with similar entropy. (PDF p. 4).
- The transposon alignment scored 7.6 standard deviations above the shuffle mean with BLOSUM 62. It scored 3.0 standard deviations above the mean with PAM 160. (PDF p. 4).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- The authors state that the matrices depend on Prosite group identity and composition and on the accuracy of PROTOMAT. (PDF p. 5).
- BLAST miss counts cannot be compared directly with FASTA or SSEARCH counts because their detection criteria differ. (PDF p. 4).
- Interpretation: the broad search evaluation is not a fully independent holdout test. Its positive groups also supplied sequences for block construction. (PDF p. 2).
