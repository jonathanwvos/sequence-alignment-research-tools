## Executive summary

- Wu and Manber extend a bit-array exact-matching method to approximate matching with insertions, deletions, and substitutions. (PDF p. 3).
- The algorithms support flexible search queries and form part of the Unix agrep package. (PDF p. 2).

## Objectives

- Provide fast, practical approximate text search with several definitions of permitted errors. (PDF p. 1).

## Arguments

- The authors argue that approximate search reduces the need to try spelling variants manually. (PDF p. 8).
- Packing pattern-prefix states into a machine word permits many states to be updated with a few bit operations. (PDF p. 2).

## Analysis

- For exact matching with a pattern that fits in one word, running time depends mainly on text length rather than text content or alphabet size. (PDF p. 2).
- Partition filtering produces fewer accidental exact matches with a large alphabet. Small alphabets increase such matches and reduce its effectiveness. (PDF p. 7).

## Methods

- A bit array records which pattern prefixes match text suffixes at the current position. Character masks mark the positions of each character in the pattern. (PDF p. 2).
- Exact matching updates the state with a shift and an AND operation. An extra OR operation can supply the initial bit when shifts fill with zero. (PDF p. 2).
- Approximate matching maintains arrays R⁰ through Rᵏ. Each array records prefix matches with at most its specified number of errors. (PDF p. 3).
- Each approximate update combines four cases: a matching character, substitution, deletion, and insertion. Deletion uses the newly updated lower-error state. (PDF p. 3).

## Applications

- Agrep searches user-defined records, such as paragraphs or mail messages, and outputs records that satisfy the query. (PDF p. 7).
- Agrep supports bounded errors, minimum-error searches, and separate nonnegative integer costs for insertions, deletions, and substitutions. (PDF p. 8).

## Optimization

- Word-level shifts and Boolean operations update multiple prefix states at once. (PDF p. 2).
- A partition-based filtering search accelerates approximate matching by using exact matches. Its performance depends on alphabet size. (PDF p. 7).

## Evaluation and testing

- The authors compare their partition algorithm with an implementation of Ukkonen's method and the Galil–Park program labeled MN2. Tests use random text of length one million, patterns of length 20, and two alphabet sizes. (PDF p. 7).
- A second test uses a 1 MB bibliographic file and queries with exact regions, wildcards, Boolean AND, union, and Kleene closure. (PDF p. 7).
- The authors also compare small regular expressions with few errors against the Myers–Miller algorithm. (PDF p. 7).

## Results

- The authors report at least a twofold speed advantage over the tested algorithms, with an order-of-magnitude advantage in many cases. (PDF p. 2).
- Searching for Homogenos with two errors in a 1 MB bibliographic text took about 0.4 seconds on a SUN SparcStation II and found a misspelling. (PDF p. 2).
- For the tested small regular expressions and error limits, the authors report that Myers–Miller took more than ten times as long. (PDF p. 7).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- The simplest bit-operation cost assumes that the pattern fits in one machine word. The authors state that other methods are better for very long patterns. (PDF p. 2).
- The authors describe the Myers–Miller comparison as unfair because that algorithm supports more general cost functions and has running time independent of the error count. (PDF p. 7).
- The work assumes unindexed files and sequential scanning. It does not address indexing for approximate search. (PDF p. 8).
