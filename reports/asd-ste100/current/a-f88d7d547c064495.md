## Executive summary

- PROTOMAT automatically finds conserved ungapped protein alignments and assembles them into compatible block sets for database searches. (PDF p. 1).
- Applied to 437 PROSITE protein groups, the system produces 1764 blocks. The blocks are calibrated against SWISS-PROT and combined into the BLOCKS database. (PDF p. 1).

## Objectives

- The system seeks the highest-scoring set of non-overlapping blocks in a consistent order across a required number of related sequences. (PDF p. 2).
- The authors aim to detect distant protein relationships and assess relationships proposed by other methods. (PDF p. 1).

## Arguments

- The authors argue that multiple block hits strengthen evidence of a relationship when their order and spacing agree with the protein family. (PDF p. 7).
- They argue that separate block scores help detect partial relationships and similarities in sequences affected by frameshifts, introns, or truncation. (PDF p. 7).

## Analysis

- The path score sums each block score multiplied by its merged-motif count. It then multiplies this sum by the fraction of sequences included in the path. (PDF p. 2).
- The lower calibration threshold is the 99.5th percentile of scores from sequences outside the construction group. The upper threshold is the median score from construction-group sequences. (PDF p. 3).
- Block strength is 1000 times the upper-to-lower threshold ratio. Search scores use the lower threshold for normalization. (PDF p. 3).

## Methods

- Modified MOTIF finds three-residue motifs with two spacing values. Sequence shuffling sets the significance threshold, and the system retains the 50 highest-scoring motif blocks. (PDF p. 2).
- MOTOMAT merges consistently overlapping motif blocks. It scores possible extensions up to width 60, selects the best extensions, and merges consistent overlaps again. (PDF p. 2).
- MOTOMAT represents blocks as graph nodes. Arcs connect blocks with compatible order and no overlap in the required number of sequences. It uses topological sorting and recursive depth-first search to enumerate paths. (PDF p. 2).
- Each block searches SWISS-PROT for calibration. For sequence queries against BLOCKS, the system converts each block to a scoring matrix during the search. (PDF p. 3).

## Applications

- The system supports both block queries against sequence databases and sequence queries against the BLOCKS database. (PDF p. 1).
- The authors use BLOCKS to investigate oxidoreductase relationships and a proposed relationship between HIV Vif proteins and thiol proteases. (PDF p. 1).
- PROTOMAT can process other groups of related proteins. Their blocks can search sequence databases or extend BLOCKS. (PDF p. 8).

## Optimization

- Before path search, MOTOMAT removes single-motif blocks whose scores are more than one standard deviation below the mean. (PDF p. 2).
- Path evaluation stops when fewer than the required number of sequences remain compatible. The required number must exceed half the input sequences. (PDF p. 2).
- Motif block scores are divided by the square root of block width to compare blocks of different sizes. (PDF p. 2).

## Evaluation and testing

- The authors compare automatic block paths with published alignments for several PROSITE protein groups. (PDF p. 3).
- For methyltransferases, six usable blocks search GenBank translated in all six frames. The test retains the top 350 entries per block and checks joint hits for strand, order, and spacing. (PDF p. 3).
- The reported timing comparison uses an 80386-20 computer with a math coprocessor. It compares searches of 510 PROSITE patterns and 1764 BLOCKS entries. (PDF p. 8).

## Results

- For 17 full-length methyltransferases, PROTOMAT selects seven blocks covering 15 proteins. The result preserves the alignments of all five previously identified highly conserved regions. (PDF p. 3).
- The translated GenBank search finds 31 entries with compatible hits from at least two blocks. All are known methyltransferases, including 11 sequences absent from the block construction set. (PDF p. 3).
- The Vif queries provide insufficient similarity to support a relationship with thiol proteases. (PDF p. 7).
- A typical PROSITE pattern search takes about 20 seconds. A comparable BLOCKS search takes about 8 minutes, but detects three oxidoreductase examples missed by the family pattern. (PDF p. 8).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- The authors caution that strong retrieval of construction sequences does not by itself establish block quality. Blocks built from random sequences can also rank their source sequences highly. (PDF p. 3).
- About 4% of blocks have strength below 1300 and need cautious interpretation. One four-column methyltransferase block has too little information for the translated database search. (PDF p. 3).
- BLOCKS depends on previously documented protein groups. The authors state that clustering-based databases can therefore represent relationships absent from BLOCKS. (PDF p. 7).
