## Executive summary

- PEbA uses contextual residue embeddings to score pairwise protein alignments. It applies dynamic programming similar to Smith-Waterman. (PDF p. 3).
- ProtT5-based PEbA had higher average alignment accuracy than the tested sequence-based methods. FATCAT had a higher average SP score on RV11. (PDF p. 7).

## Objectives

- The authors developed PEbA to improve alignment of divergent proteins, especially below 20–35% sequence identity. (PDF p. 1).
- The authors sought an embedding-based local aligner that could support remote homology searches. (PDF p. 3).

## Arguments

- The authors argue that residue identity alone does not represent the different roles of the same amino acid in different proteins. (PDF p. 2).
- The authors interpret the score distributions as evidence that embeddings detect residue similarity through context, even when amino acids differ. (PDF p. 7).

## Analysis

- Aligned residue pairs had more positive embedding scores than BLOSUM62 scores. Random residue pairs had scores centered near zero with both methods. (PDF p. 6).
- The authors examined average SP scores by sequence identity and alignment length. They omitted averages for bins with fewer than ten alignments. (PDF p. 11).
- The authors suggest that model architecture and training exposure may explain the advantage of ProtT5 over ESM-2. This explanation is tentative. (PDF p. 4).

## Methods

- PEbA extracts a 1024-dimensional vector for each residue from the last hidden layer of ProtT5-XL-U50. It removes padding and special tokens. (PDF p. 3).
- The residue match score is ten times the cosine similarity of the two embeddings. Gap opening and extension penalties are −11 and −1. (PDF p. 4).
- PEbA can generate embeddings during execution or accept existing embeddings. The authors generated all test embeddings before alignment. (PDF p. 3).
- The authors also tested ESM2-T36-3B-UR50D. This model has three billion parameters and produces 2560-dimensional residue vectors. (PDF p. 4).

## Applications

- The authors propose using PEbA to align candidate hits from embedding-based search tools such as knnProtT5. (PDF p. 15).
- The authors propose extending the scoring function to multiple sequence alignment. They did not establish its accuracy for that task. (PDF p. 13).

## Optimization

- The authors tested alternative functions of embedding similarity. These functions did not improve alignment quality, so they retained cosine similarity multiplied by ten. (PDF p. 7).
- The authors selected ProtT5-XL instead of ProtT5-XXL because prior analysis showed no performance gain from XXL and it required more embedding time. (PDF p. 4).
- The authors propose integrating the score into faster dynamic programming implementations. This is a proposed improvement. (PDF p. 13).

## Evaluation and testing

- The benchmark contained more than 12,000 pairwise alignments extracted from BAliBASE multiple sequence alignments across five references. (PDF p. 1).
- The selected references were RV11, RV12, RV911, RV912, and RV913. Their reference alignments were manually curated using structural comparisons. (PDF p. 4).
- The authors measured agreement with reference alignments using SP and F1 scores. F1 combines alignment precision and recall. (PDF p. 6).
- Comparators included BLOSUM62, DEDAL, vcMSA, and FATCAT. FATCAT was tested only on RV11. (PDF p. 7).

## Results

- On RV11, average SP scores were 0.590 for ProtT5-based PEbA, 0.336 for ESM-2-based PEbA, 0.220 for BLOSUM62, and 0.603 for FATCAT. (PDF p. 7).
- ProtT5-based PEbA had higher average SP scores than BLOSUM62, DEDAL, vcMSA, and ESM-2-based PEbA on all five references. (PDF p. 7).
- For RV11 pairs with 0–9% identity, average SP scores were 0.29 for PEbA and 0.04 for BLOSUM62. (PDF p. 11).
- For proteins 1thx and 1a8lA, PEbA achieved an SP score of 0.860. BLOSUM and DEDAL each scored zero, and vcMSA scored 0.486. (PDF p. 13).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- PEbA produced an SP score of zero for 138 of 5816 RV911 pairwise alignments. (PDF p. 11).
- The authors state that direct PEbA searches against large sequence datasets would be too slow. Practical search requires filtering or indexing. (PDF p. 15).
- The accuracy of multiple sequence alignments produced with the PEbA scoring function remains untested. (PDF p. 13).
