## Executive summary

- Wilbur and Lipman define sequence similarity through alignment fragments whose scores depend on sequence context. They state that a dynamic programming algorithm calculates this similarity. (PDF p. 2).
- The similarity score is the maximum sum of fragment significance scores minus penalties for the spaces between fragments. (PDF p. 4).
- The authors report faster database searches and lower memory requirements for long sequence alignments after three algorithm modifications. (PDF p. 10).

## Objectives

- The authors aim to make sequence context central to the evaluation of paired sequence elements. (PDF p. 2).
- They seek faster comparisons and protein alignments with greater structural significance. (PDF p. 2).
- They seek conditions that convert the context dependent similarity score into a metric distance. (PDF p. 2).

## Arguments

- The authors argue that context requirements reduce the number of candidate matches. This can reduce computation time and memory use. (PDF p. 2).
- They argue that isolated amino acid matches can lack significance. Scores based on surrounding structural similarity may produce more meaningful alignments. (PDF p. 2).
- They reject simple triplet recoding because it loses the proposed speed advantage and can make one original element serve as context at two separate alignment positions. (PDF p. 3).

## Analysis

- Fragment occurrences have a partial order. Consecutive fragments must occur in the same order in both sequences and must not overlap. (PDF p. 4).
- For finite sequences, the maximum similarity score exists and is finite. The authors state that computation time depends strongly on the size of the allowed fragment set. (PDF p. 4).
- Example 2 gives a context dependent metric construction. Identical fragments without a boundary element must have length at least 2k + 1. Interior pairs receive weight 2 only when sufficient context exists at both ends. (PDF p. 10).

## Methods

- An alignment fragment pairs two sequences of equal length without blanks. The allowed fragment set determines how context affects comparison. (PDF p. 3).
- The method selects an ordered sequence of allowed fragment occurrences between null start and end fragments. It maximizes nonnegative significance scores minus nonnegative penalties based on the two intervening sequence lengths. (PDF p. 4).
- The selected fragments define alignment features. Sequence elements and blanks are inserted between fragments to form a complete alignment. (PDF p. 4).
- For protein searches, the authors group the 20 amino acids into seven classes through cluster analysis of physicochemical properties. They also test a seven-class grouping based on the Dayhoff PAM matrix. (PDF p. 11).

## Applications

- The modified algorithm searches large sequence databases for sequences similar to a query. The authors cite the discovery of similarity between platelet derived growth factor and a transforming protein. (PDF p. 10).
- The authors use the method to align long nucleic acid sequences, including mitochondrial sequences of 10,000 bases. (PDF p. 10).
- The authors search the Georgetown Biomedical Research Foundation protein database with recoded amino acid sequences to study possible structural relationships. (PDF p. 11).

## Optimization

- The search implementation uses modifications 1–3. Modification 1 uses k = 2 for amino acid sequences and k = 4 for nucleic acid sequences, with identical sequence pairs in each allowed fragment. (PDF p. 10).
- Modification 3 further limits the size of the algorithm's set M. The authors report that this gives more than a tenfold speed increase over traditional matrix computation. (PDF p. 10).
- The authors state that computation time and memory requirements decrease rapidly as k increases. They increase k from 4 to 6 to align mitochondrial sequences of 10,000 bases. (PDF p. 10).

## Evaluation and testing

- The reported timing comparison uses two nucleic acid sequences of 500 bases, modifications 1 and 2, and k = 4. The baseline is a Needleman–Wunsch type algorithm. (PDF p. 10).
- The long-sequence timing example uses a DEC KL10 with 200 pages of physical memory and 300 pages of virtual memory. (PDF p. 10).
- The authors evaluate selected protein relationships through Monte Carlo shuffling of the involved sequences. They compare significance at k = 2 and k = 1. (PDF p. 11).
- They examine crystallographic data for the aligned regions of Bovine trypsinogen and Rhodopseudomonas capsulata cytochrome C' after a search with PAM-based classes and k = 3. (PDF p. 11).

## Results

- For sequences of 500 bases, modifications 1 and 2 take about one quarter of the average time required by the Needleman–Wunsch type baseline. The authors report no detected sensitivity loss after adding modification 3. (PDF p. 10).
- The authors report an optimized alignment of two nucleic acid sequences of 2,000 bases in less than 10 seconds on the specified DEC KL10 system. (PDF p. 10).
- For selected relationships from physicochemical class searches, Monte Carlo significance is much greater at k = 2 than at k = 1. The authors interpret this as evidence of a context effect. (PDF p. 11).
- The PAM-based search finds a statistically significant relationship between Bovine trypsinogen and cytochrome C'. The authors report strong three-dimensional structural similarity in the aligned regions. (PDF p. 11).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- The fragment sequence does not uniquely determine the complete alignment. Elements between fragments can have different arrangements. (PDF p. 4).
- The authors state that many long-range protein interactions cannot be analyzed through the small contexts that are practical to use. (PDF p. 11).
- The biological significance of several statistically significant protein relationships remains uncertain. The authors state that further understanding of protein structure is needed. (PDF p. 11).
- The reported studies use similarity scores. The authors have not investigated whether context dependent metrics are useful for evolutionary distance or for other string comparison applications. (PDF p. 11).
