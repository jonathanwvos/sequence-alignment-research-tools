## Executive summary

- Malign aligns ungapped nucleic-acid sequences by maximizing total sequence information in a selected window. (PDF p. 2).
- Repeated runs from random starting alignments produce solution classes. The authors use their information values and frequencies to assess convergence. (PDF p. 1).

## Objectives

- Find useful multiple alignments when exhaustive search is impractical and conservation is spread across binding sites. (PDF p. 1).

## Arguments

- The authors select information content because it supports further information analysis and a two-state thermodynamic model of binding. (PDF p. 2).
- The authors argue that consensus sequences discard base-frequency information that Malign retains. (PDF p. 7).

## Analysis

- The authors state that computational order is proportional to sequence count, window width, and allowed shift range. They attribute practical speed to rapid convergence. (PDF p. 7).
- The authors interpret distinct Fis alignment peaks as evidence of self-similarity with spacings of 7 and 11 bases. (PDF p. 6).

## Methods

- The method tabulates base counts and frequencies at each window position. It computes uncertainty with a small-sample correction and sums information across the window. (PDF p. 3).
- Each sequence is tested at all allowed shifts. The shift with highest information is retained, with pseudo-random selection between ties. (PDF p. 3).
- Passes repeat across the sequences. One stated stopping condition is a complete pass with no alignment changes. (PDF p. 3).
- Completed alignments are stored as shift vectors. Repeated vectors are counted, and solutions are sorted by information content. (PDF p. 6).

## Applications

- The authors apply Malign to OxyR and Fis binding sites, where weak conservation at individual positions makes manual alignment difficult. (PDF p. 7).
- Malin converts alignment vectors into Delila instructions for further processing and sequence-logo display. (PDF p. 6).

## Optimization

- Lookup tables avoid repeated evaluation of expensive functions, including logarithms. (PDF p. 1).
- The authors identify alignment steps, sums within steps, and table precomputation as candidates for parallel processing. (PDF p. 7).

## Evaluation and testing

- The OxyR test used 16 binding sequences, a 35-base window, and shift settings from −100 to +100. The authors compared 1,000 and 10,000 runs. (PDF p. 6).
- The Fis test used 44 collected binding sites, a 21-base window, and shifts from −6 to +6. Tests included 1,000 runs and two sets of 10,000 runs with different random seeds. (PDF p. 6).

## Results

- The OxyR tests found the same frequently recovered best alignment at 13.9 bits. The 10,000-run test also found three alignments near 13.4 bits. (PDF p. 6).
- Fis required 103 seconds for 1,000 runs on a Sun SPARCstation 20/61. The two 10,000-run tests required 1,023 and 1,014 seconds and gave almost identical results. (PDF p. 6).
- The best Fis alignment was separated from lower-scoring alignments by about 1 bit. Some lower-scoring alignments occurred more often, which the authors interpret as easier discovery rather than higher quality. (PDF p. 6).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- Malign does not introduce gaps. The authors identify variable binding-site spacing as a reason that ungapped alignment can be insufficient. (PDF p. 7).
- Shift-vector normalization was not implemented in version 2.38. Equivalent vectors made the reported OxyR distribution approximate. (PDF p. 6).
- The Fis shift restriction did not prevent the algorithm from finding nearby binding sites. (PDF p. 6).
- The authors leave gap treatment unresolved. They discuss possible information-based penalties but identify conceptual and computational difficulties. (PDF p. 8).
