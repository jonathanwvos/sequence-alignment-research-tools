## Executive summary

- EVfold infers residue couplings from multiple sequence alignments with a maximum entropy model. It converts these couplings into distance constraints for protein structure generation. (PDF p. 3).
- The authors report blinded structure predictions for 15 proteins. For 12 families, the top-ranked models have Cα-RMSD errors of 2.7–4.8 Å over at least 75% of residues. (PDF p. 7).

## Objectives

- The authors test whether inferred evolutionary couplings identify residue proximity and provide enough distributed contacts to predict protein folds without known structures. (PDF p. 2).
- The study aims to assess the structural information in evolutionary couplings. It does not aim to optimize performance across all available prediction methods. (PDF p. 15).

## Arguments

- The authors argue that local correlation measures cannot separate direct coupling from transitive correlation. A global model addresses interdependencies among residue pairs. (PDF p. 3).
- The authors conclude that pairwise co-evolution statistics can contain enough information to recover a native fold. Their tested approach does not explicitly require higher-order couplings. (PDF p. 13).

## Analysis

- Matrix inversion gives the mean field coupling calculation its global character. The authors contrast this with mutual information, which produces more clustered and redundant predicted contacts. (PDF p. 13).
- Contact accuracy alone does not determine fold accuracy. The distance from each observed contact to its nearest predicted contact also correlates with structural accuracy. (PDF p. 11).
- Adding constraints initially reduces structure error. Further constraints can reduce accuracy as false positives accumulate. The required number varies with the protein family. (PDF p. 11).

## Methods

- The maximum entropy model fits pair frequencies from an aligned protein family while keeping sequence probabilities maximally even under those constraints. (PDF p. 3).
- Mean field direct coupling analysis estimates residue couplings. Selected couplings become distance constraints for distance geometry, energy minimization, and molecular dynamics refinement. (PDF p. 3).
- The method down-weights sequences with more than 70% residue identity to family neighbors. The reported effective sequence counts are approximately 12–40% of the available counts. (PDF p. 13).
- Blind ranking uses helix and beta-sheet twist criteria, weighted by secondary structure content. Candidate structures with knots are removed. (PDF p. 7).

## Applications

- The test set includes soluble proteins and a transmembrane receptor. The predictions use sequence information without structural templates or fragment libraries. (PDF p. 3).
- The authors propose using predicted structures to assist crystallography and NMR, identify remote structural homology, and prioritize structural genomics targets. These are proposed applications. (PDF p. 15).

## Optimization

- Evolutionary distance constraints restrict the conformational search space. Energy minimization and molecular dynamics refine the generated structures. (PDF p. 3).
- The constraint-count experiments suggest that approximately 0.5–0.75 predicted constraints per residue can support reasonable fold prediction. The authors report substantial variation between proteins. (PDF p. 11).
- For blind ranking, the authors routinely generated candidates with up to one long-range constraint per aligned residue. They advise against larger constraint sets because false positives can degrade predictions. (PDF p. 13).

## Evaluation and testing

- The blinded test covers 15 proteins with 48–258 residues and different folds. Predicted structures are compared with experimental structures. (PDF p. 3).
- Contact evaluation counts true and false predictions against known structures. A contact is defined as a minimum interatomic distance below 5 Å. Fully correct experimental constraints provide a control for the structure-generation pipeline. (PDF p. 9).
- The authors compare global direct information and Bayesian network models with local mutual information and statistical coupling analysis. They also generate structures from Bayesian network constraints for ten proteins. (PDF p. 11).
- Retrospective tests use archived Pfam alignments. They generate 20 structures for each tested constraint count and examine the best structure as sequence availability changes. (PDF p. 13).

## Results

- The top-ranked Elav4 model has 2.9 Å Cα-RMSD over 67 of 71 residues. The top-ranked Ras model has 3.6 Å Cα-RMSD over 161 residues. (PDF p. 6).
- The top-ranked trypsin model has 4.3 Å Cα-RMSD over 186 of 223 residues. The top-ranked rhodopsin model has 4.84 Å Cα-RMSD over 171 residues. (PDF p. 7).
- For HRAS, the true-positive fraction exceeds 0.8 among the top 50 contact predictions and 0.5 among the top 200. For ELAV4, the corresponding fractions exceed 0.7 and 0.4. (PDF p. 9).
- The global models yield higher contact accuracy than the two local models. Bayesian network constraints generally give larger structure errors than direct information constraints in the reported comparisons. (PDF p. 11).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- Incomplete alignment coverage removes coupling information for omitted regions. It can also reduce prediction accuracy within the retained region. (PDF p. 11).
- Even fully correct constraints produce errors of approximately 2 Å Cα-RMSD or more. Standard structural metrics can also miss incorrect chain topology. (PDF p. 9).
- False contacts can arise from sparse sampling, phylogenetic bias, or functional constraints unrelated to proximity within a monomer. The main study requires at least 1000 aligned family sequences to reduce noise. (PDF p. 15).
- Removing knotted candidates excludes genuinely knotted proteins. The membrane-protein implementation also lacks membrane-orientation information. (PDF p. 7).
