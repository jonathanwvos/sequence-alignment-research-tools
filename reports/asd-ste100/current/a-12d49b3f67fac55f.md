## Executive summary

- The authors organize next-generation sequence assembly into four stages: preprocessing filtering, graph construction, graph simplification, and postprocessing filtering. (PDF p. 1).
- They propose a layered assembler architecture that supports data from different sequencing platforms. (PDF p. 16).

## Objectives

- The authors aim to identify computational challenges and describe tools and algorithms for each assembly stage. (PDF p. 1).

## Arguments

- Early error correction can reduce graph size. Correction during graph simplification can address errors that become visible only after graph construction. (PDF p. 3).
- The authors argue that defined inputs, outputs, and communication between layers can support assembler development. (PDF p. 17).

## Analysis

- Frequency-based correction assumes that erroneous reads are rare. Genomic repeats and uneven sampling can produce several equally plausible corrections. (PDF p. 3).
- Assembly quality depends on the dataset, assembly algorithm, k-mer size, and minimum overlap length. The authors describe a tradeoff between contiguity and accuracy. (PDF p. 14).

## Methods

- K-spectrum filters weight k-mers by frequency and base quality. They separate trusted and untrusted k-mers with a threshold and seek corrections with few editing operations. (PDF p. 5).
- Suffix tree or suffix array filters count substrings of variable length. They seek similar candidates for rare substrings and remove affected reads when correction candidates are absent. (PDF p. 5).
- Multiple sequence alignment filters align candidate erroneous reads with trusted reads to infer consensus bases. Coral and ECHO implement this approach. (PDF p. 5).
- PBcR aligns short reads to long PacBio reads and seeks a maximum matching to construct a consensus sequence. (PDF p. 5).

## Applications

- Reference-based assembly aligns fragments to a related genome for tasks such as resequencing. De novo assembly reconstructs genomes without this guidance. (PDF p. 2).
- Paired-end read alignments provide contig orientation and insert-size constraints. These constraints filter a contig connectivity graph during scaffolding. (PDF p. 14).

## Optimization

- The authors state that parallel error correction during graph construction can reduce total computation time. (PDF p. 3).
- The proposed modular architecture can use Bloom filters, FM-indexes, and sparse k-mers. Modules can be replaced without changes to other modules. (PDF p. 17).

## Evaluation and testing

- Alignment to a reference genome can assess reference coverage, contig accuracy, long-range contiguity, and insertion, deletion, and substitution patterns. (PDF p. 14).
- The authors describe Assemblathon metrics for haplotype consistency and correct contiguity. They also describe GAGE E-size as the expected contig or scaffold length containing a randomly selected reference base. (PDF p. 14).
- Feature-Response Curves use paired-end read alignments to assess the tradeoff between assembly contiguity and accuracy. (PDF p. 14).
- The N50 example sorts contigs by length and accumulates their lengths until half the total is reached. For lengths of 20, 15, 10, 5, and 2 kb, N50 is 15 kb. (PDF p. 16).

## Results

- The authors report that a cited evaluation found Reptile, HiTEC, and ECHO gave the best substitution-correction results among the assessed methods. Coral performed better than Hybrid-SHREC for indel correction. (PDF p. 5).
- A cited study found benefits from separate correction tools for ABySS, SOAPdenovo, Velvet, and CABOG. SGA performed best with its built-in correction module. (PDF p. 5).

## Limitations and uncertainty

- Correction tools still face problems with automatic parameter selection, polymorphism detection, varied error models, and increasing time and memory demands. (PDF p. 5).
- The authors call for more assessment of separate correction tools against built-in correction modules. (PDF p. 5).
- The proposed general assembler specifies layers and services. Implementation details are left to developers. (PDF p. 17).
